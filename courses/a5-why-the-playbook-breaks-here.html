Why the data-science playbook breaks here

A data team that has shipped a churn model, a demand forecast or a credit score carries a playbook it no longer notices: pull the data, join it on an ID, wait for the label, ship, monitor, retrain. Every step of it assumes something about the world. A market quietly withdraws five of those assumptions: where the data comes from, what a row stays attached to, how clean the answer is, whether the target reads your model, and when being wrong starts to cost. Marlow Street, a constructed research desk, is about to review a strategy pitch with that playbook in hand. Tonight is where each step breaks, drawn as a mechanism, with the generator's measured figures beside it. Synthetic data only; nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Marlow Street, the day the old playbook is opened on a new desk

Marlow Street is a constructed desk: four researchers, one head, a budget for two strategies a year, and a backtested Sharpe ratio of 1.8 that has now been through four sessions of questions. The head came from a product data team. Her instinct, when a pitch arrives, is to run it through the review she ran for every churn and credit model she ever signed off: where does the data come from, what is the label, how will we monitor it, what happens if it is wrong. Every one of those questions is still the right question. What changes is the answer, and the answer changes for a reason that can be drawn.

★ What you walk out with tonight. Five contrasts between a conventional data-science project and a trading strategy, each drawn as the mechanism rather than a slogan: the source, the identity, the ground truth, the feedback loop and the cost of being wrong. The measured size of three of them on a generator whose truth is known, with the trial count beside every figure, and the published measurement of a fourth. LiveFive places the playbook assumes something3 min▶

Take any model a data team ships, a churn model is the cleanest, and list what it quietly relies on. Each item below is an assumption that holds there and fails here, and each failure has a mechanism that the three Parts draw.

  • The source. A churn model gets a fresh cohort of customers every month. A strategy gets one history, shared with everyone, that grows by one day per day.
  • The identity. A churn row is a customer with an ID who is still that customer next month. A market has no row that stays attached to anyone; the other side of a trade is whoever turns up that day.
  • The ground truth. A churn label is a recorded fact: the customer left or stayed. A strategy's label is a return, and its signal-to-noise ratio is close to zero.
  • The feedback loop. A churn customer never reads the model. A market does: once a signal is found and traded, the edge it measured shrinks.
  • The cost of being wrong. A wrong churn score wastes a voucher and is found next quarter. A wrong trade pays its cost on the day, and the size of the position decides whether the mistake is survivable.
Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Reflexive. Acting on a prediction changes the data the prediction was built from. Session 2 named it; Part 1 draws the loop.
  • Decay. The shrinking of a measured edge after it is found. McLean and Pontiff (2016) published its size for 97 predictors.
  • Independent history. Observations that are not reruns of the same days. It cannot be resampled into existence; it arrives at one day per day.
  • Signal-to-noise. How large the effect is against the spread of its estimate. For a Sharpe ratio on two years of daily data, the spread is about 0.71 by formula.
  • Turnover. How much the position changes per day. A cost is charged on the change, not on the holding.
  • Capacity. How much money a strategy can run before its own trades move the price against it. Named tonight, not measured: the generator has no price impact.
  • Leverage. Position size as a multiple of capital. At leverage 6, a 1 percent move against you is a 6 percent loss.
  • Paper result. What the backtest reports. The live result is what the same rule earns with real costs, a real size and a market that has read the paper.
  • Constructed. Marlow Street, the pitch and every figure from the generator are invented so that the truth is known. No figure describes a real market.
Part 1 · covers the identity and the feedback loop

The target fights back

10 min live

A churn model works on a target that holds still. The customer it scores is the same person next month, and nothing about the score changes what that person would have done, unless you act on it on purpose and measure the effect with a hold-out. A trading signal works on a target that does neither. There is no person on the other side to hold still, and the crowd that is there can find the same signal and trade it away. The figure draws the two contrasts as four panels: what a row stays attached to, and whether the target reads you.

LiveThe identity: a churn row keeps its ID, a market has no row4 min▶

Most of a data team's craft rests on the join. Customer C-1042 has a history, a segment, a tenure and a label, and every one of those can be attached to the same ID next month. When the model is wrong about C-1042, you can look at C-1042 and ask why. A credit model works the same way with an applicant, and a demand forecast with a store and a product.

A trading strategy has nothing to join on. The return on a day is the net result of everyone who traded that day, for their own reasons, and tomorrow it is a different crowd with different reasons. There is no entity whose behaviour you are modelling, only an aggregate whose members change without telling you. That has a practical consequence a leader should hear in plain words: you cannot debug a wrong prediction by looking at who did what. The unit of analysis is the pattern, and a pattern has no identity to inspect.

It also changes what "the same model, next month" means. A churn model retrained on next month's data is learning about the same customers plus some new ones. A strategy re-estimated on next month's data is learning about a crowd that may have changed its mind, which is session 2's non-stationarity seen from the data team's side.

LiveThe feedback loop: an offer changes a customer, a trade changes the price4 min▶

A churn model does have a feedback loop, and a good team designs it. The model scores customers, the offer goes to the high scorers, and a hold-out group gets no offer so that the effect of the offer can be measured. The customer is changed by the offer on purpose, and nobody on the customer side has read the model. The loop is closed by the team, through an experiment, which is the subject of the experimentation sessions.

In a market the loop is closed by somebody else. A signal is found, published or simply discovered independently, and other people trade it. Their trades move the price towards where the signal said it would go, earlier and harder, and the edge that was measured gets smaller. McLean and Pontiff measured this across 97 published predictors: "Portfolio returns are 26% lower out-of-sample and 58% lower post-publication". They attribute the difference to traders learning from the papers: "We estimate a 32% (58%-26%) lower return from publication-informed trading". The Journal of Finance 71(1), 2016, read at source in the published version.

The generator behind every session is stationary: its signal is as strong on the last day as on the first, and nobody reads it. So the bench cannot show decay, and no number on these pages is a decay measured on the generator. The published figures are the evidence, and the mechanism is the loop in the figure.

Real world

Marlow Street's head (constructed) asked the question she always asked of a churn model: "What is the monitoring plan?" The researcher had one: re-estimate the rule each quarter. She pointed at the loop. Re-estimating tells you the edge shrank after it has shrunk. The question for a strategy is how many other desks could have found the same rule from the same public history, and the honest answer for a rule built on two years of shared data is "any of them".

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

How much does an edge decay once it is found? McLean and Pontiff's published headline is 26 percent lower out of sample and 58 percent lower after publication, across 97 variables, in The Journal of Finance, 2016. Earlier drafts of the same study are reported, from summaries of working-paper abstracts that were not read at source, to have used a different number of characteristics and to have given other percentages. The estimate moved as the sample grew. That is normal for a study of a moving target, and it is the reason to quote the published figures with the journal and the year rather than as a constant.

Decayed or gone? The published study measures a smaller average edge after publication, not a vanished one, and it measures it over many predictors, not for any one. Some readers take that as proof that published signals still pay; others take the 26 percent pre-publication drop, which is there before anyone has read the paper, as a sign of how much of any in-sample edge was the search rather than the world. Both readings fit the same numbers. A leader does not need to settle it to act on it: a pitch should state what decay it assumes and why, and a pitch that assumes none has picked a side without saying so.

Zero or worse? A separate open question sits underneath: whether an overfit rule earns nothing afterwards or less than nothing. Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado and Zhu (2014) say zero for a series without memory and worse for a series with memory. The generator has no memory, so overfit rules earn zero here, not less than zero.

Part 2 · covers the source and the ground truth

You cannot rerun the experiment

9 min live

A churn model reruns itself. Every month a new cohort arrives, the model scores it, and a month later the labels come in and say how it did. There is no A/B test of a trading strategy, and there is no next cohort either: there is one history, everyone has the same copy, and the label it gives back is a return whose noise dwarfs the effect. The figure draws both contrasts, where more data comes from and how clean the answer is, with the generator's measurements on the right.

LiveThe source: a new cohort every month, against one shared path3 min▶

When a churn model looks shaky, the fix is often to wait: next month's cohort is a fresh, independent sample from the same process, and in a year there are twelve of them. The data is also yours. Nobody else has your customers' records, so whatever you find in them is yours to use.

A backtest has neither property. The history is one realised path, the same path every other desk has, and the only way to get more of it is to wait for it to happen, at one day per day. The generator puts a number on what that costs. Plant one real strategy with a Sharpe ratio of 1.5 among 99 that know nothing, select the best on the training years, and test it with the deflated Sharpe ratio. On 3 years of data the planted one passes in 3 of 100 trials; on 6 years, 6; on 12 years, 45; on 24 years, 94. Over all of those runs, every strategy that passed was the planted one, so the test made no false discoveries; it simply had no power until the history was long.

This is the generator's version of what Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado and Zhu (2014) call the minimum backtest length: the number of years a search of a given size needs before its best result means anything. The only input that moves it is independent history, and independent history cannot be manufactured.

LiveThe ground truth: a label you can read, against one you mostly cannot3 min▶

A churn label is about as clean as data gets. A month after the score, the customer either left or stayed, and the record says which. The noise in a churn project is in the model, not in the label. A credit label takes longer to arrive, but when it arrives it is still a fact about one borrower.

A strategy's label is its return, and the signal-to-noise ratio of that label is close to zero. One estimate of an annualised Sharpe ratio on 503 daily observations, roughly two years, has a spread of 0.71 by formula when there is nothing there at all; measured over 200 generated null worlds the spread is 0.70. A single rule reporting 1.0 sits about one and a half spreads from zero. Sharpe's 1994 paper gives the same fact from the other side: "The t-statistic will equal the Sharpe Ratio times the square root of T", and T on two years is small.

Put that noise beside the selection of session 3 and the result is stark. A real Sharpe of 1.5 hidden among 100 rules on three years passes the deflated test in 0 of 200 trials in one ladder of the generator, 1.5 percent of 200 in a second, and 3 percent of 100 in a third that uses fresh seeds. "Almost never", not "never": the label is not silent, it is just very quiet against the search.

Real world

Marlow Street's head (constructed) offered the researcher what she would have offered a churn team: "Run it live for a quarter and we will look at the result." A quarter is about 63 trading days. If two years of data give a spread of 0.71, a quarter gives a spread several times wider by the same formula, so the live quarter could not confirm or refute a 1.8 whatever it showed. She kept the idea and changed what it was for: a quarter of paper running checks the plumbing, the costs and the fills, not the edge.

Self-studyWhy there is no A/B test of a strategy3 min▶

An A/B test works because a random draw splits one population into two groups that live through the same period, so the only difference between them is the treatment. A strategy has no population to split. There is one market and one period, and the strategy either ran in it or did not. Running it on half the money and not the other half does not help, because both halves face the same prices on the same days; the only thing the split measures is the size of the position. The experimentation sessions teach the A/B test properly, and the difference is the one session 1 started with: an A/B test can be rerun, a backtest cannot.

Resampling is the other tempting fix. A bootstrap draws thousands of reshuffled histories from the one you have, and every one of them is built from the same days. It can show how unstable an estimate is on that history; it cannot add the history you do not have, and the deflated test does not treat it as if it did. The figure's last line is the short version: resampling reshuffles one history, it does not add a second one.

Part 3 · covers costs, capacity and sizing

The paper result is not the live result

8 min live

When a churn model is wrong, the cost is a voucher sent to a customer who would have stayed anyway, and the team finds out next quarter and retrains. The error is small, late and reversible. When a trading rule is wrong, the cost lands on the day: every change of position pays a cost whether the rule was right or not, and the size of the position decides whether a run of bad days is a dent or the end. The figure draws where the cost is charged, then puts the generator's measurements of costs and size side by side.

LiveCosts are charged on the position change3 min▶

A strategy's paper result is usually gross: it assumes every change of position was free. The generator charges a cost per unit of position change, which is the simplest honest model, and runs one real strategy with a Sharpe ratio of 1.0 at six cost levels, 200 worlds each. Out of sample it reads 1.05 at no cost, 0.98 at 1 basis point, 0.93 at 2, 0.74 at 5, 0.43 at 10 and -0.23 at 20. In sample the pattern is the same, ending at -0.30.

The arithmetic is short enough to do in a meeting. On this world the position flips on about 20 percent of days (the sign of the hidden signal changes with probability 0.205), so turnover is about 0.41 units a day, and each 5 basis points costs about 0.32 of annualised Sharpe. A real edge of 1.0 is gone by 15 basis points and negative at 20. Nothing about the edge changed; it was eaten one flip at a time.

The churn analogue would be a model whose every score, right or wrong, cost money to act on, charged the moment it was acted on. Few data teams have run one, which is why the habit of reading a gross number survives the move.

LiveCapacity and slippage: what the generator cannot show2 min▶

A flat cost per flip is the floor, not the bill. Two more costs grow with size. Slippage is the gap between the price the backtest assumed and the price the trade actually got. Capacity is the size beyond which a strategy's own trades move the price against it, so that each extra unit of money earns less than the last and, past some point, less than nothing. A churn model has no equivalent: scoring ten times as many customers does not make each score worse.

The generator has no price impact. Its cost per flip is the same at any size, so it cannot measure slippage or capacity, and no number on these pages is either one. Execution and market microstructure are out of scope by design. What a leader can do without them is ask the pitch for the size it was tested at, the size it is asking to run at, and what it assumed about the difference. A pitch that tested at one size and asks for ten has a capacity assumption whether it wrote one down or not.

LiveSizing: the mean says lever up, the median says stop3 min▶

The same real 1.0, cost-free, compounded over three years at rising leverage, 200 paths each. At leverage 1 the median path ends at 1.57 and none lost half its value at any point. At leverage 6 the median is 4.80, the highest growth rate on the ladder, and 98.5 percent of paths lost half at some point along the way. At leverage 12 the mean is 71.48 and still rising, while the median has fallen back to 1.45. At leverage 24 the median path ends at 0.00 to two decimals and the growth rate per year is below -4.

Leverage 6 is the growth peak because it is the full-Kelly fraction for this world: a daily mean of 0.00063 over a daily variance of 0.0001 gives 6.3. Past it, more size buys a higher mean, carried by a few extraordinary paths, and a lower median, which is what a typical path actually lives through. Never read the mean column without the median beside it.

Real world

Marlow Street's pitch (constructed) had a slide titled "Scaling" showing the average outcome at four times the tested size. The head asked for one more line on the same slide: the median, and the share of paths that lost half on the way. A credit team would recognise the move. It is the difference between the expected loss of a portfolio and the loss the institution has to survive in a bad year, and in a strategy the bad year is chosen by the size, not by the market.

Self-studyWhy "assist, never advise" is the same rule here2 min▶

Every figure in this Part is a property of a generator with a planted signal of known strength: a cost level, a size, a median. None is a property of anything anybody can trade, and none is a recommendation to use any cost assumption or any leverage. The rule held in Markets and investing with AI, assist, never advise, applies unchanged: what a leader takes from here is a way of reviewing a claim, not a view on any market.

Part 4 · build-along

A leader's review of a strategy pitch

13 min hands on

No code. The pitch is constructed: the deck Marlow Street's researcher would bring to fund the 1.8, written the way such decks are usually written. You review it the way you would review a churn model's launch deck, except that each of your five questions is now the contrast it came from. The figure is the review card; pin it to the deck.

Lay out the constructed deck

Six slides, as Marlow Street's researcher wrote them: a headline Sharpe ratio of 1.8 on a two-year backtest; an equity curve that rises steadily; "robust across parameter settings"; "costs assumed negligible"; "monitoring: re-estimate quarterly"; and "scales to four times the tested size". Write each slide's claim on its own line, and leave space under each for the question it owes.

Ask how many things were tried

"Robust across parameter settings" means parameter settings were tried. Count them, with the rules and features that were dropped, and write N. Then hold the headline against it: the best of 100 rules that know nothing reads 1.80 in sample on two years and earns nothing afterwards (200 trials). If N is unknown, write "unknown" under the headline and stop reading it as a result.

Ask which split, and whether it respected time

Was the test period after the training period? Did observations overlap, and if so, was the overlap purged? A shuffled split on five-day observations reads 0.65 out of sample on a world with no alpha at all (100 worlds). Write the split down in one sentence; session 4's leak audit has the rest.

Ask which costs, at what turnover

"Costs assumed negligible" is a number of zero. Ask for the cost per unit of position change and the number of changes per day, and multiply. On the generator, a real 1.0 that flips on about 20 percent of days is gone by 15 basis points and reads -0.23 at 20 (200 worlds). Then ask about size: what was the cost assumption at the size it asks to run at, and who checked capacity?

Ask what decay is assumed, and what size and drawdown

"Monitoring: re-estimate quarterly" assumes the edge holds still between estimates. Ask what decay the pitch assumes, and point at the published figures: 26 percent lower out of sample and 58 percent lower after publication across 97 predictors (McLean and Pontiff 2016). Then go to "scales to four times": ask for the median path and the share of paths that lost half at the proposed leverage, not the mean.

Write the one-page review

Five lines, one per question: answered, answered badly, or unanswered. Under them, one sentence on what would have to be true for the 1.8 to survive all five, and one on what the desk should ask for next. Unanswered counts as a finding, never as a pass. The review is a judgement on a research claim, not a view on any market.

The habit to keep. Keep the playbook's questions and throw away its default answers. Where does the data come from, what is the label, who reads the model, what does being wrong cost: ask every one of them of a strategy pitch, and expect the answers to be one shared history, a label that is mostly noise, a crowd that reads, and a cost charged on every flip at a size the deck chose. A pitch that answers as if it were a churn model has not yet been reviewed.

Check yourself

Three questions

A churn model keeps its accuracy for years with quarterly retraining. A published return predictor does not. Which mechanism separates them?

A churn customer never reads the model; a market's other side does, and its trades move the price the signal was measured on. Noise and overfitting are real but they are not the loop. The 26 percent drop before publication is the part a search leaves behind; the further fall after publication is the part they attribute to publication-informed trading.

Marlow Street's researcher says the fix for a weak result is "more data". What is the honest constraint?

A bootstrap reshuffles the same days and adds no independent history, and shuffling overlapping observations leaks (0.65 on nothing in session 4). The only input that moves the test's power is more history, and it cannot be bought faster than it happens.

A strategy with a real Sharpe ratio of 1.0 flips its position on about 20 percent of days. Its deck says "costs assumed negligible". What does the generator say?

A cost is charged on every change of position, at any size, so a small edge with high turnover is eaten one flip at a time. Size adds slippage and capacity on top, which the generator does not model. Costs land in and out of sample alike: -0.30 and -0.23 at 20 basis points.

Homework

Twenty minutes, on a model your own team has shipped and on the review you just wrote.

Run the five contrasts on one of your own models

Pick a churn, demand or credit model your team runs. For each of the five, the source, the identity, the ground truth, the feedback loop and the cost of being wrong, write one line on what your model relies on. Mark the ones that would break first if the target started reading the model.

Sort your habits into three piles

From your one-page review, list the habits from your old playbook that helped, the ones that were missing, and the ones that pointed the wrong way, such as reading a gross figure or trusting a quarter of live data. Session 6 turns the three piles into a transfer map.
