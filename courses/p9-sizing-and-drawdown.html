Sizing and drawdown: risk is the product

Sessions 3 to 8 asked whether an edge is real and what is left of it after costs and planted faults. Tonight takes an edge that is real, a Sharpe ratio of exactly 1.0 planted in the generator, and changes only one thing: how much of it is held. Same signal, same 200 worlds, eight leverages. The mean outcome keeps climbing long after the typical outcome has started to fall, the growth rate peaks at one leverage and then collapses, and the leverage that maximises growth here turns out to be a number you can work out from two constants of the generator. The data is synthetic throughout, and nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Marlow Street decides how much

Marlow Street is a constructed research desk, four people and a head who has been handed a backtest with a Sharpe ratio of 1.8 and asked whether to fund it. Every earlier session asked a version of "is it real?". Tonight asks the question that comes after a yes: at what size? The answer is not a footnote to the strategy. Two desks holding the same real edge at different sizes are running two different products, with different typical outcomes, different worst stretches and a different chance of ever reaching the end. Everything here is measured on synthetic worlds, and nothing here is investment advice.

★ What you walk out with tonight. One measured ladder, a real Sharpe of 1.0 compounded over three years at eight leverages, 200 worlds per rung: the mean terminal wealth beside the median at every rung, so you can see them part (14.57 against 4.80 at leverage 6, 71.48 against 1.45 at 12). The maximum drawdown and the share of paths that lost half at some point, which is already 98.5 percent at leverage 6. The growth rate per year, which peaks at 0.502 at leverage 6 and is negative by 16. The arithmetic that puts the peak there: daily mean over daily variance, 0.00063 over 0.0001, about 6.3, the Kelly fraction of this world. And what happens to the same worlds when they are sized for the 1.8 a best-of-100 search reports instead of the 1.0 that is really there.

LiveWhy the size is part of the strategy4 min▶

A data scientist who ships a model usually ships one version of it. A strategy ships with a dial on it, the leverage, and every setting of that dial is a different distribution of outcomes. The Sharpe ratio does not change when the dial turns, because leverage multiplies the mean and the standard deviation alike. Almost everything a person living through the result cares about does change: how much it grows in the typical case, how far it falls on the way, and whether it survives the worst stretch.

That is the sense in which risk is the product. The edge decides whether there is anything to sell. The size decides what is being sold, and a desk that reports the edge without the size has described the ingredient, not the dish.

Real world

Marlow Street's head, constructed like the rest of the desk, asked two researchers for a sizing proposal on the same rule. One proposed the leverage with the highest average outcome; the other proposed the leverage with the highest typical outcome. They were three rungs apart on the ladder below, and both had computed their numbers correctly. They had answered different questions, and only one of them was the question the desk would live with.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Leverage. How many units of the rule are held per unit of wealth, rebalanced to the same multiple every day. Each day wealth is multiplied by 1 plus leverage times that day's return on the rule.
  • Terminal wealth. What one unit of starting wealth has become after 755 daily observations, three years of the generator.
  • Mean terminal wealth. The average of that number across 200 worlds. Pulled up by the few paths that compound furthest.
  • Median terminal wealth. The middle path when the 200 are sorted. What a typical world ends with.
  • Drawdown. On any day, one minus wealth over the highest wealth reached so far. The maximum drawdown is the worst of those on one path.
  • Lost half. A path whose maximum drawdown reached 0.5 at some point, whatever it did afterwards.
  • Growth rate per year. The log of terminal wealth, times 252, over the number of observations. The pace a single path compounds at.
  • Kelly fraction. Here, the leverage that maximises the growth rate in this stated synthetic world: daily mean over daily variance. A property of the world, not a recommendation.
Self-studyWhere tonight sits among the ten sessions3 min▶

Sessions 1 and 2 built the null and the strategy; 3 and 4 measured selection and corrected for it; 5 cleaned the splits; 6 planted a real signal; 7 charged it for trading; 8 planted survivorship and lookahead in the data and caught them. Tonight assumes all of that went well and the edge is a real 1.0, then asks how much of it to hold. Session 10's bench drives every ladder at once, including this one.

Sizing is where a backtest most easily turns into advice, so the rule here is the same one that governs AI in markets generally: assist, never advise. The Kelly fraction below is the growth peak of one generated world with known constants. It is not a size for anything real.

LiveWhy the mean runs away from the median4 min▶

Wealth compounds by multiplication, so terminal wealth is a product of 755 daily factors. A product of many factors is skewed: a handful of paths that string together good days end far above the rest, and nothing can end below zero. The median is the middle of that skewed pile and follows the growth rate of a typical path. The mean adds the far tail on top, and the tail gets longer as leverage stretches every daily factor further from 1.

In expectation each day multiplies wealth by 1 plus leverage times a positive mean, so the true mean of terminal wealth keeps rising with leverage. The 200-world mean does not: it reads 71.48 at 12, then 62.27 at 16 and 1.04 at 24. That is not the true mean turning down. It is the mean becoming unmeasurable, because the paths that carry it are too rare to appear in 200 draws. A quantity that falls when the thing it estimates is rising cannot be the number a desk plans on.

Real world

Marlow Street's first researcher, constructed, proposed leverage 12 on the strength of the mean: 71.48, the highest on the table. The head asked for the median beside it. It was 1.45, below the 1.57 of leverage 1. The proposal had sized for the best few worlds and would have handed the desk one of the typical ones.

The Sharpe ratio of the rule is 1.0 on every rung. Nothing about the edge distinguishes these eight products; only the size does.

Self-studyequity(), line by line4 min▶

The engine's sizing is eight lines, and each one is a decision worth knowing about.

  • w starts at 1, peak at 1. Every path begins with one unit of wealth, which is also its first high-water mark.
  • w *= 1 + leverage * y[i]. Daily rebalancing to a constant multiple. There is no cost on the rebalance; session 7's cost is not charged here, which flatters every rung and the high ones most.
  • If w falls to zero or below, it is zero and the path stops. Wealth cannot go negative here, and a path that hits zero has a maximum drawdown of 1.
  • peak and drawdown. The running maximum, and one minus wealth over it, kept as the worst value seen.
  • growth = log(w) * 252 / n. The per-year compounding pace of this one path. A path at zero has no log; sizingRung counts it as -10 a year so the mean can still be taken. That floor is why the leverage 24 growth must be read as "below -4", not as a measured return.
  • sizingRung sorts the 200 terminals and takes the 101st. That is the median it reports, beside the mean, the mean maximum drawdown and the share with a drawdown of at least 0.5.
Part 2 · covers drawdown, the half-loss share and the Kelly peak

What a single path lives through, and where growth peaks

12 min live

A desk does not receive the mean of 200 worlds. It receives one path, and it lives through every day of it. Two numbers describe that path better than any average: how far it fell from its best, and how fast it compounded. The second one has a peak, and in this world the peak sits at a leverage you can compute before running anything.

LiveDrawdown, and the share of paths that lost half4 min▶

A path that falls by half has to double to get back to where it was. A path that falls by three quarters has to quadruple. That arithmetic is why drawdown is measured from the running peak and not from the start: it is the hole the path is standing in, and the size of the climb out.

Read the ladder's drawdown columns with that in mind. At leverage 1 the mean maximum drawdown is 0.18 and no path lost half. At 2 it is 0.34 and 8 percent lost half. At 4 it is 0.58 and 72.5 percent lost half. At 6, the growth peak, it is 0.75 and 98.5 percent lost half. From 8 upward every one of the 200 paths lost half at some point. The rule is a real 1.0 on every rung; the experience of holding it is not.

This is why a leader reads the median and the drawdown and not the mean. The mean describes a portfolio of 200 independent desks, which nobody runs. The median describes the desk you are likely to be. The drawdown describes whether that desk is still allowed to be running by the end: a limit, an investor or a nerve gives out somewhere on the way down, and the generator has no model of which.

LiveKelly as the growth peak of this world4 min▶

Per day, a path at leverage L grows by about L times the daily mean, minus L squared times the daily variance over two. The first term is the edge, scaled. The second is the cost of volatility to a compounding path, and it grows with the square of the leverage. Their difference is a hump with its top where the two balance: leverage equals daily mean over daily variance.

In this world both are known. A real Sharpe of 1.0 at a daily volatility of 0.01 is a daily mean of 0.00063, and the daily variance is 0.0001. The ratio is about 6.3, the full Kelly fraction of this world. The ladder measures the peak at leverage 6, at 0.502 a year; by the same arithmetic the peak growth is the Sharpe ratio squared over two, about 0.5 a year, so formula and measurement agree.

Past the peak the squared term wins. Growth is 0.466 at 8 and 0.080 at 12; by the arithmetic it returns to zero near twice the Kelly fraction, about 12.6, and the ladder has it at -0.750 by 16. At 24 the rung's growth figure is a mean that includes paths floored at -10 a year when wealth hit zero, so it reads as below -4 and nothing finer.

Two cautions keep this honest. The 6.3 is the growth peak of a stated synthetic world whose mean and variance are set by hand; it is not a size for any real strategy, whose mean is never known. And a growth peak is not a recommendation even here: at leverage 6, 98.5 percent of paths lost half on the way.

LiveSizing on an overstated Sharpe4 min▶

The Kelly arithmetic needs the edge as an input, and sessions 3 and 4 showed what the reported edge usually is: the best of many. The best in-sample Sharpe of 100 rules with no alpha at all is 1.80 on average over 200 worlds (ladder A), and its out-of-sample figure is -0.06. Marlow Street's 1.8 sits exactly there.

Suppose the rule is real but its true Sharpe is 1.0, and the desk sizes for the 1.8 it was shown. Full Kelly for 1.8 in this world is 1.8 times 6.3, about 11.3 by the arithmetic, and the nearest canon rung is 12. On the same 200 worlds the median terminal wealth falls from 4.80 at the true peak to 1.45, growth from 0.502 a year to 0.080, and every path lost half. The mean at 12 is 71.48, the highest on the ladder, so a report that showed only the mean would make the overstated sizing look like the best decision on the table.

Selection inflates the edge from the top; sizing on the inflated edge then multiplies the damage. If the 1.8 were pure selection, the edge would be zero and any leverage would be sizing nothing. That rung is not in the canon; the homework runs it.

Real world

Marlow Street's head, constructed, put one line on the sizing memo: size on the out-of-sample estimate, deflated, and show the median and the half-loss share at that size and at the size the in-sample number would imply. The gap between those two columns, 4.80 against 1.45 on the ladder, was the cost of believing the backtest.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Is the growth peak the right target at all? One camp holds that over a long enough horizon the leverage that maximises growth ends ahead of any other with near certainty, so it is the natural reference point and everything else is a deliberate step back from it. The other camp answers that nobody has an infinite horizon, that a desk is judged on a finite path with limits on the way, and that the right size comes from how much drawdown can be survived, not from where growth peaks. Both are internally consistent. They disagree about the objective, and no measurement on this page settles an objective.

Full or fractional? By the growth formula the hump is symmetric in leverage, so sizing well below the peak gives up little growth while cutting drawdown a great deal. The ladder shows the shape: leverage 4 keeps 0.436 of the peak's 0.502 a year and 72.5 percent of paths lost half; leverage 2 keeps 0.268 and 8 percent lost half. One view treats a fraction of Kelly as insurance against an overstated edge, which the previous card showed is the usual case. Another treats it as an admission that the edge estimate was never good enough to size on, and asks for a better estimate instead. Which fraction, if any, is not settled here.

What this bench cannot show. The generator has normal returns, constant volatility, no fat tails, no regimes and no memory, and the rebalance is free. Real drawdowns at a given leverage depend on exactly the features left out, and are usually described as worse. That is a stated limit of the bench, not a measured figure.

Part 3 · build-along

Compound it by hand, reproduce the ladder, size on the wrong number

18 min hands on

Open this page's console. The engine is loaded as QT. You will write the compounding loop yourself, check it against the engine on one world, reproduce ladder E at the canon seeds, find the growth peak against the arithmetic, and then size the same 200 worlds for the 1.8 a best-of-100 search reports. Every step uses the canon seeds, so your numbers will match for the same seed. The same loop is ten lines in plain Python if you prefer it there; the homework asks for that port.

Make the first canon world and take the planted rule

Ladder E's first world is seed 90,001 with a true Sharpe of 1.0. The planted rule is the world's own hidden signal, traded with h of 1 and no cost, which is exactly what sizingRung does. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

consolevar w = QT.makeWorld({ trueSR: 1, seed: 90001 }); var y = QT.pnl(w, w.signal, { h: 1 }); y.length

Write the compounding loop yourself

Multiply wealth by 1 plus leverage times each day's return, stop at zero, keep the running peak and the worst drawdown. Then compare with the engine on the same world at leverage 6. The two terminal values and the two drawdowns should be identical to the last digit, because they are the same arithmetic in the same order.

consolefunction myEquity(y, L) { var v = 1, peak = 1, dd = 0; for (var i = 0; i < y.length; i++) { v *= 1 + L * y[i]; if (v <= 0) { v = 0; dd = 1; break; } if (v > peak) peak = v; dd = Math.max(dd, 1 - v / peak); } return { terminal: v, maxDrawdown: dd }; } [myEquity(y, 6), QT.equity(y, 6)]

One world at every leverage, and why it is not the ladder

Run the one world at all eight leverages and print its realised Sharpe ratio beside them. One world is one draw: its realised Sharpe is not 1.0, and its row of terminals is not the canon. Read it for shape only, then move on to 200 worlds.

consolevar L8 = [1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24]; [QT.annualise(QT.moments(y).sr, 1).toFixed(2), L8.map(function (L) { return myEquity(y, L).terminal.toFixed(2); })]

Reproduce ladder E at the canon seeds

sizingRung runs the same loop over seeds 90,001 to 90,200 and reports the mean and median terminal wealth, the mean maximum drawdown, the share that lost half and the growth per year. It prints the canon rows exactly: 1.61 and 1.57 at leverage 1, 14.57 and 4.80 at 6, 71.48 and 1.45 at 12. All eight rungs take well under a second.

consoleL8.map(function (L) { var r = QT.sizingRung(L, 200, 1, 90000); return [L, r.meanTerminal.toFixed(2), r.medianTerminal.toFixed(2), r.meanMaxDrawdown.toFixed(2), r.shareHalved.toFixed(3), r.meanGrowthPerYear.toFixed(3)]; })

Find the peak, and check it against the arithmetic

Compute the daily mean of a real 1.0 from the generator's constants, divide by the daily variance, and print the growth formula, L times the mean minus L squared times the variance over two, per year, beside the measured column. The Kelly fraction prints about 6.3 and the formula's peak about 0.5 a year, beside the measured 0.502 at leverage 6. Past the peak the formula and the ladder drift apart a little, because the formula is an approximation and the ladder is 200 worlds.

consolevar mu = 0.01 / Math.sqrt(252), v = 0.0001; [(mu / v).toFixed(2), L8.map(function (L) { return [L, ((L * mu - L * L * v / 2) * 252).toFixed(3), QT.sizingRung(L, 200, 1, 90000).meanGrowthPerYear.toFixed(3)]; })]

Size the same worlds for a claimed 1.8

The real edge stays at 1.0. Only the belief changes: full Kelly for a Sharpe of 1.8 is 1.8 times 6.3, about 11.3, and the nearest canon rung is 12. Print the two rungs side by side. The median prints 4.80 at 6 and 1.45 at 12, the growth 0.502 and 0.080, and the share that lost half 0.985 and 1.000. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

consolevar claimed = 1.8 * mu / v; [claimed.toFixed(1), [6, 12].map(function (L) { var r = QT.sizingRung(L, 200, 1, 90000); return [L, r.meanTerminal.toFixed(2), r.medianTerminal.toFixed(2), r.meanGrowthPerYear.toFixed(3), r.shareHalved.toFixed(3)]; })]

The habit to keep. Never report a size without the median and the drawdown beside the mean, and never size on an edge you have not deflated. The mean flatters every leverage, and the in-sample Sharpe flatters every edge; together they recommend the worst row on the table.

Check yourself

Three questions

At leverage 12, over 200 worlds, the mean terminal wealth is 71.48 and the median is 1.45. Which should a desk plan on, and why?

A · The mean, because it is the expected value and a desk should maximise expected value
B · The median beside the drawdown, because a desk lives one path, the mean is carried by a few paths that compound furthest, and at 12 every path lost half on the way
C · Neither, because leverage 12 has the same Sharpe ratio as leverage 1 and so the outcomes are equivalent
The mean describes 200 independent desks pooled, which nobody runs. The typical path at 12 ends at 1.45, below leverage 1's 1.57, after a drawdown that reached half on every one of the 200 paths. The Sharpe ratio is unchanged by leverage, which is exactly why it cannot tell these products apart.

Growth per year peaks at leverage 6 (0.502) and is negative by 16 (-0.750). Why does it fall past the peak?

A · Because the planted edge weakens at higher leverage
B · Because the engine charges a cost on every rebalance
C · Because growth is about L times the daily mean minus L squared times the daily variance over two; the edge term grows with L, the volatility term with its square, and past mean over variance, about 6.3 here, the square wins
The edge is 1.0 on every rung and sizingRung charges no cost. What changes is the volatility drag on a compounding path, which grows with the square of the leverage. The peak is where the two terms balance, the Kelly fraction of this stated world.

A rule's real Sharpe is 1.0, but the desk sizes it at full Kelly for the 1.8 its best-of-100 backtest reported. On ladder E's 200 worlds, what happens?

A · The size is about 11.3, the nearest canon rung is 12, and the median falls from 4.80 to 1.45 while growth falls from 0.502 to 0.080 and every path lost half
B · Nothing much, because Kelly is robust to errors in the edge
C · The desk does better, because the mean at 12 is the highest on the ladder
Full Kelly scales with the believed edge, so an inflated edge buys an inflated size. Here it lands near twice the true peak, where growth is close to zero. The mean at 12, 71.48, is the highest on the ladder, which is exactly why a mean-only report would endorse the mistake.

Cheat sheet

The one sentence. The edge decides whether there is a product; the size decides what the product is.
The pair. Never the mean without the median. At leverage 12: 71.48 and 1.45, 200 worlds.
The hole. Drawdown is measured from the running peak. Losing half means doubling to get back.
The share. Paths that lost half: 8 percent at 2, 72.5 at 4, 98.5 at 6, all of them from 8.
The hump. Growth per day is about L times the mean minus L squared times the variance over two.
The peak. Mean over variance: 0.00063 over 0.0001, about 6.3. Measured peak 0.502 a year at 6.
The trap. Size on an in-sample 1.8 and you land near twice the true peak: median 1.45, not 4.80.
The limit. Normal returns, constant volatility, free rebalancing. A growth peak of a stated world, never advice.
