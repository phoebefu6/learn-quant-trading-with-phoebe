Learn Quant Trading with Phoebe · Leader session 2 of 6

The market is not a customer

A data team's models are scored against people who never see them. A churn model does not make a customer leave sooner because it flagged her. A trading signal is scored against counterparties who are looking for the same signal, and once enough of them find it and trade it, the edge it measured shrinks. Tonight is two facts a leader needs before funding any strategy: the market reads you back, and on two years of data the noise in one Sharpe estimate is almost as large as the edge anyone hopes to find. Marlow Street, the constructed desk from session 1, is still deciding. Synthetic data only, and nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Marlow Street, a week after the 1.8 landed

Marlow Street is a constructed four-person research desk, invented so that every number about it can be checked. Last session its head learned to ask how many rules were tried before the 1.8 was presented. This week the researcher has an answer and a new argument: the signal comes from a published paper, so it must be real. The head's instinct, trained on customer models, says a published result is a safer result. In markets it can be the opposite, and the reason is the subject of Part 1.

★ What you walk out with tonight. Three words that separate a market from a customer base: adversarial, reflexive, non-stationary. The published measurement of what happens to a return predictor after it is found and printed: 26 percent lower out of sample, 58 percent lower after publication, across 97 variables. A ruler for a Sharpe ratio, 0.71 on two years of daily data, and a measured reason why a real 1.0 cannot be confirmed on that much history. And five questions to put to anyone who says a signal will keep working. Synthetic data only; nothing here is advice on what to buy, sell or trade.

LiveWhy "published" sounds safe and is not3 min▶

In product analytics, a published result is a reusable one. If a paper shows that onboarding emails reduce churn, your customers do not read the paper and change their behaviour to defeat it. The finding transfers because the people it describes are not playing against you.

A return predictor describes the behaviour of other traders, and other traders read papers. Publication is the moment a private edge becomes a public one, and a public edge is one that many desks can trade at once. The leader's instinct "it was peer reviewed, so it holds" is right about the statistics and wrong about the world: peer review can confirm the effect was there in the sample, and cannot stop the sample's participants from learning about it.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Adversarial. The thing being predicted contains people trying to predict it, and they profit when your prediction is wrong or crowded.
  • Reflexive. Acting on a prediction changes the data it was built from. Trading a signal moves the returns the signal was measured on.
  • Non-stationary. The process generating the data changes over time, so the past is a sample from a different distribution than the future.
  • Decay. The shrinking of a measured edge after it was found. McLean and Pontiff (2016) put published numbers on it.
  • Out of sample, in their sense. The years after the original study's sample ended and before the paper came out. Not the same as a held-out test set.
  • The ruler. The standard deviation of one annualised Sharpe estimate when nothing is there: about 0.71 on 503 daily observations.
  • Constructed. Marlow Street and every figure from the generator are invented so the truth is known. No figure describes a real market.
Part 1 · covers adversarial, reflexive, decaying

The adversary reads the paper too

15 min live

McLean and Pontiff took 97 variables that published academic work had shown to predict returns and followed each one through three windows: the years the original study used, the years after its sample ended but before the paper came out, and the years after publication. If a predictor were a law of nature, the bar would stay the same height in all three. The figure draws what they measured instead, in the published version of the paper, The Journal of Finance 71(1), 2016.

LiveThe adversary reads you5 min▶

A churn model's customers never read the model. They do not know it exists, they do not change their behaviour because it scored them, and nobody earns money by making it wrong. That is why a data team can validate a churn model once and trust it for a year: the population it describes is indifferent to it.

A trading signal's counterparties are not indifferent. Every trade has someone on the other side, and that someone is also searching for patterns, often with the same papers, the same data and the same methods. When a pattern is found by many, they all trade it, and the price moves before any one of them can collect. The edge does not vanish because the original analysis was wrong. It shrinks because the analysis was right and other people did it too.

Real world

Marlow Street (constructed) has a researcher who built the 1.8 strategy from a predictor described in a well cited paper. Her argument to the head was that publication proves the effect. The head's new question is the right one: if it is in a paper, how many other desks read the same paper, and in which of the three windows of the figure are we about to start trading? The answer is the third window, the one with the shortest bar.

LiveReflexivity: trading changes the series4 min▶

In most modelling the data and the decision are separate. The model reads the data; the decision happens downstream and does not rewrite the history the model learned from. In markets the decision is a trade, and the trade is part of the next day's data. Act on a signal at scale and you change the returns the signal was measured on. That loop is what the dashed arrow in the figure draws.

McLean and Pontiff measured the loop's size from the outside. Across 97 variables, "Portfolio returns are 26% lower out-of-sample and 58% lower post-publication". They attribute the difference to traders learning from the papers: "We estimate a 32% (58%-26%) lower return from publication-informed trading". The study was read at source, in the published version in The Journal of Finance, 2016.

Notice what the first drop is. The 26 percent fall between the sample and the pre-publication years happened before anyone could trade on the paper. It is what remains when a search across many variables is scored on the data it searched, which is session 1's best-of-N lesson seen in published research. Session 3 measures that part on the generator.

Self-studyNon-stationarity, and why the bench cannot show it3 min▶

A stationary process has the same statistical behaviour at every point in time. Markets are not one: participants, rules, costs and crowding all change, so the edge measured in one decade is a sample from a different process than the one you will trade in the next.

The generator behind every session is stationary on purpose. Its planted signal has the same strength on day 1 and day 756, nobody reads it, and nothing trades against it. That is what lets the bench say what the truth is, and it is also why the bench cannot reproduce decay. Every decay figure tonight comes from the published study, not from the generator, and a page that claimed otherwise would be inventing evidence.

The generator also has no memory in its returns, so an overfit rule earns zero out of sample here, not less than zero. On a series with memory, Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado and Zhu describe a compensation effect that can make it worse. The bench is the no-memory case and says so.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

How much decay? The published paper's headline is 26 percent out of sample and 58 percent after publication. Earlier drafts of the same study reported other figures (82 characteristics, and 25 and 56 percent in a later draft); those draft figures are reported from abstract summaries, not read at source. The honest statement is that the estimate moved as the sample grew, and the published numbers are the ones to cite, with the journal and the year.

Why the decay? The paper splits the decline into two parts. The first, out of sample but before publication, is the part a statistician would call bias from searching. The second, the extra 32 percent, the authors attribute to publication-informed trading, arguing that "investors learn about mispricing from academic publications". The 32 is a difference between two estimates, not a separately measured quantity, so how cleanly the split holds is a fair question to put to it. These pages teach both parts and do not settle which dominates in any market today; no figure here could.

Part 2 · covers signal-to-noise near zero

A ruler of 0.71, and a real 1.0 that cannot be seen

10 min live

Decay is the market's reason a signal stops working. Noise is the statistics' reason you cannot tell whether it was working in the first place. Sharpe's 1994 paper gives the link in one line: "The t-statistic will equal the Sharpe Ratio times the square root of T". On two years of daily data T is small, so one estimate of a Sharpe ratio carries a standard deviation of about 0.71 annualised on 503 daily observations by formula (0.70 measured over 200 worlds). Hold a real 1.0 against that ruler and it is about 1.4 rulers from zero.

LiveSharpe's own ruler4 min▶

Sharpe's 1994 paper says two things a leader should carry. First, the ratio "is not independent of the time period over which it is measured", which is why a daily figure is annualised by the square root of 252 before anyone compares it. Second, "The t-statistic will equal the Sharpe Ratio times the square root of T", with T the number of observations. Turn that around and the noise in one estimate falls only with the square root of the history you have.

On 503 daily observations, roughly two years, one annualised estimate on a series with nothing in it has a standard deviation of 0.71. That is the ruler. A real Sharpe ratio of 1.0, a strong edge by most standards, is 1.0 divided by 0.71, about 1.4 rulers from zero. A one-sided 95 percent test needs about 1.17 on the same history before it calls anything a finding, so a real 1.0 sits below the line its own estimate has to clear.

The generator counts the other side. On the same 200 null worlds of ladder A, with one rule and nothing there, the in-sample estimate came out above 1.0 in 15 and above 1.17 in 12. A desk that sees a single 1.0 on two years is looking at a number that pure noise passed in 15 of those 200 worlds, and that a real 1.0 is centred on. Two years cannot tell them apart.

Real world

Marlow Street (constructed) has a second proposal on the desk: one rule, tried once, no selection at all, with a two-year Sharpe ratio of 1.0. The head, freshly wary of searches, is inclined to fund it because N is honestly 1. The ruler says the decision is still a coin the desk cannot see: on 200 null worlds, 15 produced a figure above it. Funding it may be right, but it is a bet on a prior, not a conclusion from the data, and the head now says so in the memo.

LiveTwo hundred worlds against one3 min▶

Set the box to "nothing" and it prints the canon: a mean in-sample Sharpe ratio of -0.01 over 200 worlds, and an out-of-sample mean of -0.07. Set it to "a real 1.0" and the means recover the truth: 1.01 in-sample and 1.05 afterwards, with no cost charged. Averaged over 200 worlds, the generator always finds what is there.

That is precisely the luxury a desk does not have. The bench gets 200 histories; the market gives one. The third line in the box is the share of single worlds in which the estimate cleared a one-sided 95 percent test. Under "nothing" it is the false-positive rate. Under "a real 1.0" it is how often one two-year history would have confirmed a real edge. Read it, and notice how far it sits from certainty.

The seeds are the canon seeds, so at 200 worlds the box prints the canon figures to the last digit: 10,000 for nothing, 70,000 for a real 1.0 alone at no cost.

Part 3 · build-along

Read a signal like an adversary

17 min hands on

No code. Five questions Marlow Street's head now puts to any claim that a signal will keep working, in the order the figure draws them. Each is a gate: a written answer opens it, a missing answer is the finding. Run them on the published-predictor proposal from Part 1, then on a model your own team relies on.

How was it found?

Ask for the search, not the result. One stated hypothesis tested once, or the best of a sweep? Write down N, including the variants that were thrown away. Session 1's lesson applies unchanged, and McLean and Pontiff's 26 percent pre-publication drop is what a search leaves behind even in refereed work.

Has it been published, or traded?

If the signal is in a paper, a conference talk or a widely shared notebook, assume other desks read it too. Ask which of the three windows in the Part 1 figure the desk would be trading in. For a published predictor it is the third, the one with the 58 percent lower bar, and the proposal should say so in its first paragraph.

How many years of history?

Ask for the number of daily observations behind the headline, and whether any of them were used to choose the rule. The ruler shrinks only with the square root of the history, so it takes four times the years to halve it. Independent history is the one input nobody can manufacture.

How many rulers out?

Divide the headline Sharpe ratio by 0.71 if the history is about two years. A real 1.0 comes out at about 1.4 rulers, under the 1.17 line its own estimate must clear for a one-sided 95 percent test. Say the result out loud and remember it only holds if N at gate 1 was 1; any search makes it worse, as session 3 measures.

What would make it stop working?

Ask the presenter to name the mechanism of decay before anyone funds the strategy: who else could trade it, what would crowd it, what change in costs or rules would end it. A researcher who can name it can also monitor it. One who answers "nothing" has told you the claim is untested against an adversary.

The habit to keep. Read every signal as the people on the other side of the trade would read it. Ask who else has seen it, how long it was measured, and how many rulers it stands from zero. A customer model can be right and stay right. A trading signal that is right gets found, and a found signal gets traded away.

Check yourself

Three questions

A signal that worked in a published study earns less once Marlow Street starts trading it. Why is that not a bug in the model?

A · Because the model was trained on too few features and needs retraining
B · Because markets are random, so no signal can ever work
C · Because the counterparties read the same studies and trade the same signal, and their trading changes the returns it was measured on
The market is adversarial and reflexive. A churn model's customers never read the model; a trading signal's counterparties do. The decay is the world responding to a correct finding, not an error in fitting it. Option B overstates: the published study measures a smaller edge, not none.

McLean and Pontiff (2016) report returns 26 percent lower out of sample and 58 percent lower after publication. What do the two numbers separate?

A · The 26 percent falls before anyone could trade on the paper, so it reflects the search itself; the further 32 percent (58 minus 26) is what the authors attribute to publication-informed trading
B · The 26 percent is trading costs and the 58 percent is taxes
C · They are two estimates of the same thing from two different samples
The windows are the years after the original sample but before publication, and the years after publication. The first drop is bias from searching; the extra 32 percent is the authors' estimate of what readers of the paper traded away. The published figures are the ones to cite; earlier drafts reported others.

A single rule, tried once, shows a Sharpe ratio of 1.0 on two years of daily data. Why can that history not confirm a real 1.0?

A · Because a Sharpe ratio below 2 is never meaningful
B · Because one estimate on 503 daily observations has a standard deviation of about 0.71, so a real 1.0 is only about 1.4 rulers from zero, and on 200 null worlds the estimate reached 1.0 in 15
C · Because the rule must be tested on more rules first
Sharpe 1994: the t-statistic equals the Sharpe ratio times the square root of T. On two years the ruler is 0.71, the one-sided 95 percent line is 1.17, and pure noise produces 1.0 or more often enough that one history cannot sort them. Option A invents a threshold; option C confuses selection with noise.
Before session 3

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one model or claim your own team depends on and on the guess you committed to in session 1.

Run the five gates on one claim

Pick a model or finding your team treats as stable. Write one line per gate: how it was found, who else could know it, how much history, how many rulers out (or the nearest equivalent for its metric), and what would make it stop working. Mark the gates nobody could open.

Name your model's adversary, if it has one

Fraud, pricing, bidding and ranking models all face people who adapt to them. Customer churn and demand forecasts mostly do not. Write which kind yours is, and one sentence on what an adapting counterparty would do to it.

Revisit your Marlow Street guess

Your single number for what the 1.8 earns in the year after funding. Would you lower it now, given the ruler and the published decay? Write the new number beside the old one; session 3 checks both against the generator.
