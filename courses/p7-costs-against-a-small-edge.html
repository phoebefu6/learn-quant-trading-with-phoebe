Costs against a small edge

Session 6 planted a real signal and asked whether the corrections keep it. Tonight assumes they did, and asks the next question a desk has to answer before it funds anything: what is left after the rule pays to trade? The engine charges a cost on every change of position, and a signal that persists for about five days still changes position often enough for that cost to matter. Tonight reads the one line that charges it, derives turnover from the signal's persistence, and walks a real Sharpe ratio of 1.0 up a cost ladder until there is nothing left. The data is synthetic throughout, and nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Marlow Street's gross number

Marlow Street is a constructed research desk, four people and a head who has been handed a backtest with a Sharpe ratio of 1.8 and asked whether to fund it. Sessions 3 to 6 asked what that number was the best of, whether its held-out days were really unseen, and whether a real edge would survive the correction. Tonight asks a plainer question that none of those answered: was the 1.8 computed before or after the rule paid to trade? The backtest's own footnote said "gross". That one word is the subject of tonight.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. An exact reading of where the engine charges a cost: on each change of position, so a flip from long to short pays twice and a hold pays nothing. The turnover of a signal derived from its persistence alone: with phi of 0.8 the sign changes on 20.5 percent of days, which is about 0.41 units of position change a day. The arithmetic that turns that into a Sharpe ratio: about 0.32 per 5 basis points. One measured ladder, a real Sharpe of 1.0 with no competition over 200 worlds per rung, that reads 0.69 at 5 basis points, 0.37 at 10 and -0.30 at 20. And the habit of never reading a gross figure without the turnover beside it.

LiveWhy a gross Sharpe ratio is half a sentence4 min▶

A data scientist who ships a model rarely pays per prediction in a way that changes whether the model is worth shipping. A strategy pays every time it changes its mind. The fee, the spread and the slippage are charged on the trade, not on the prediction, so the same signal can be worth funding at one cost per trade and worth nothing at another. A gross figure tells you how often the rule was right. Only the net figure tells you whether being right paid.

Selection, the subject of sessions 3 and 4, shrinks a false edge from the top: the number you saw was the best of many and the truth was lower. Costs shrink a real edge from below: the number you saw was true, and then a tax came out of every trade. Both arrive at the same place, a figure that looked fundable and is not, from opposite directions.

Real world

Marlow Street's head, constructed like the rest of the desk, asked the researcher two questions about the 1.8: how often does the position change, and what does a change cost? The researcher had neither number. The backtest had been written to measure whether the signal was right, and it had never been asked to pay for acting on it.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Basis point. One hundredth of a percent: 0.0001. A cost of 5 basis points is 0.0005 of the position's value per unit traded.
  • Unit of position change. The absolute change in the position from one day to the next. Opening from flat to +1 is one unit; a flip from +1 to -1 is two.
  • Turnover. Units of position change per day, averaged over the history. Here about 0.41.
  • Gross and net. The strategy's observations before and after the cost is subtracted. Every figure on a desk should say which one it is.
  • Spread. The gap between the price at which you can buy and the price at which you can sell at the same moment. Crossing it is a cost of trading.
  • Slippage. The difference between the price when the decision was made and the price actually obtained. Also a cost of trading.
  • Per-trade cost. Here, spread, slippage and fees collapse into one number, costBp, charged per unit of position change. Nothing finer is modelled.
  • Capacity. How much can be traded before the cost per trade itself rises. Named tonight, not modelled.

Part 1 · covers where a cost is charged and turnover

A cost on every change, and how often the position changes

12 min live

The engine charges cost in exactly one place, and it is not where most people first guess. It does not charge for holding a position, and it does not charge per day. It charges for the distance the position moves. So the total cost of a rule is the cost per unit times the number of units it moves, and the number of units is a property of the signal: how persistent it is decides how often its sign changes.

LiveThe one line that charges a cost4 min▶

QT.pnl(world, signal, opts) walks the observations in time order. At observation t it takes the position from the sign of the signal, pos = signal[t] >= 0 ? 1 : -1, adds up the next h returns into fwd, and writes one number:

y[t] = pos * fwd - cost * Math.abs(pos - prev), with cost = costBp / 10000, and then prev = pos.

  • A hold. pos equals prev, the absolute difference is 0, and the observation is charged nothing, however long the position is held.
  • An opening. prev starts at 0, so the very first observation pays one unit, |+1 - 0| or |-1 - 0|. Once per history, then never again.
  • A flip. From +1 to -1 the absolute difference is |-1 - (+1)| = 2. Closing the long and opening the short are two units, and the line charges both on the day of the flip.

That is the entire cost model. It does not know what the trade was for, how large it was, or how busy the day was. It knows the distance the position moved, and it charges a fixed amount per unit of that distance.

LiveTurnover from persistence: 0.205 flips, 0.41 units4 min▶

The rule trades the sign of an AR(1) signal with phi of 0.8. Today's value and yesterday's are two standard normals with correlation 0.8, and the probability that two such normals have different signs is arccos(phi) divided by pi. With phi of 0.8, arccos(0.8) is 0.6435 radians, and over pi that is 0.205. The position flips on about 20.5 percent of days, so it lasts about five days on average: 1 / 0.205 is just under 5.

Each flip is two units, so turnover is 2 times 0.205, about 0.41 units of position change a day. The opening adds one unit across the whole history, which over 755 observations is too small to see.

Notice what turnover does not depend on: the strength of the edge. The dial that sets the true Sharpe ratio sets the weight the returns put on the signal, not how often the signal changes sign. A rule with a real 3.0 and a rule with a real 0.5 trading signals of the same persistence pay the same cost. The edge is the numerator; the cost comes out of it at a rate fixed by phi.

Real world

Marlow Street's researcher, constructed, had described the 1.8 rule as "a slow signal" because its positions lasted a few days. The head asked for the turnover in units a day. A position that lasts five days on average is a flip every five days, two units each, and that is 0.4 units a day of paid trading. "Slow" had been a description of the chart, not of the bill.

LiveEach 5 basis points costs about 0.324 min▶

Put the two numbers together and the cost can be read in the same units as the edge.

  • Per day. 0.41 units a day times 0.0005 per unit (5 basis points) is 0.000205 of return taken out of the average observation.
  • In Sharpe terms, per day. The rule's daily observations have a standard deviation of about 0.01, because the world's daily volatility is one percent. 0.000205 over 0.01 is 0.0205 of a daily Sharpe ratio.
  • Annualised. Times the square root of 252, about 15.87: 0.0205 times 15.87 is 0.325. About a third of a Sharpe point per 5 basis points.

The ladder agrees. A real 1.0 reads 1.01 in-sample with no cost and 0.69 at 5 basis points, averaged over 200 worlds: a drop of 0.32. The arithmetic is linear in the cost, so 10 basis points takes about 0.65 and 20 takes about 1.3, and a real edge of 1.0 crosses zero at about 15 basis points. The cost comes almost entirely out of the mean; the standard deviation barely moves, because the charge is tiny next to a one percent daily return.

Self-studyWhy the break-even is a ratio of two numbers3 min▶

Rearrange the arithmetic and the break-even cost is the edge divided by the cost of one basis point in Sharpe terms. At 0.41 units a day, one basis point costs 0.41 times 0.0001 over 0.01 times 15.87, about 0.065 of annualised Sharpe. A real 1.0 divided by 0.065 is about 15 basis points.

Two levers move it, and only two. A larger edge pushes break-even up in proportion. Lower turnover pushes it up in inverse proportion: halve the flips and the same edge survives twice the cost. Nothing else in the backtest helps. A longer history narrows the error bars on the net figure but does not change where it crosses zero, and no amount of selection makes a rule cheaper to trade.

Part 2 · covers the cost ladder against a real 1.0

Walk a real edge up the cost ladder

12 min live

Take the planted strategy alone: one rule, trading the world's hidden signal, with a true Sharpe ratio of 1.0 and no competitors, so there is no selection to correct and the only thing that moves the number is the cost. Walk-forward, 503 days in-sample and 252 out, 200 worlds per rung, at costs of 0, 1, 2, 5, 10 and 20 basis points per unit of position change. Every edge here is real by construction. The ladder shows how much of it a desk would keep.

LiveReading the ladder4 min▶

Every rung is the same 1.0 rule with no competitors, 200 worlds, seeds 70,000 plus the cost plus 1 onward. Read it from the top.

  • 0 basis points. 1.01 in-sample, 1.05 out of sample. The engine measures the planted 1.0 correctly when nothing is charged.
  • 1 and 2. 0.95 and 0.98; 0.88 and 0.93. Each basis point takes about 0.065, as the arithmetic says.
  • 5. 0.69 and 0.74. A third of the edge is gone, at a cost most people would call small.
  • 10. 0.37 and 0.43. Most of it is gone.
  • 20. -0.30 and -0.23. The rule is right more often than not, on every one of these worlds, and it loses money after paying to act on it.

Two things are missing that sessions 3 and 4 trained you to look for. There is no gap between in-sample and out of sample beyond trial noise, because there is only one rule and so no selection to correct. And the out-of-sample column is not lower than the in-sample one; it is a little higher on every rung, which is the same 200 worlds' test days happening to be kinder than their training days, not a property of costs. The only thing moving these numbers is the bill.

Real world

Marlow Street's head, constructed, took the ladder to the funding meeting with one sentence on it: even if the 1.8 were real, which sessions 3 and 4 gave reason to doubt, a rule that flips every five days loses a third of a Sharpe point for every 5 basis points it pays to trade, and the backtest had charged none. The researcher was asked to rerun it net, at a cost per trade the desk could defend, before anyone discussed the number again.

LiveSpread, slippage and capacity, as far as this bench goes4 min▶

Spread and slippage are two kinds of per-trade cost, and here they are one number. Crossing the spread costs the gap between the buying and selling price; slippage costs the move between the decision and the fill; fees cost what they cost. In this engine all three are folded into costBp, charged per unit of position change, the same on every day and for every size. That is enough to teach the arithmetic of turnover against an edge, which is the point of tonight.

What is not covered. Execution, order books and market microstructure: how an order is worked, how the spread and slippage arise, how they vary through the day and with conditions. None of it is modelled, and none of it is taught here. A per-trade number with a slider is as fine as this bench goes.

Capacity is named, not modelled. In practice the cost of a trade rises with its size, so an edge that survives at one size can vanish at a larger one. This engine charges the same basis points whatever the size, so it cannot say at what size any edge disappears, and no figure for capacity appears on this page. Treat any net Sharpe ratio, here or anywhere, as true only at the size its cost assumption was made for.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Trade less, or trade cheaply? One camp optimises the signal for lower turnover: smooth it, slow it, hold positions longer, so the same cost per trade is paid less often. The case is the arithmetic above, where halving the flips doubles the cost an edge can survive. The other camp keeps the faster signal and works on paying less per trade, on the grounds that a slower signal trades less but also says less: smoothing throws away the recent information that made it right. Which effect wins depends on the signal, and is open. On this bench the dial that sets the edge does not depend on phi, so slowing the signal halves the bill and keeps the whole edge; that is a property of the generator, and it cannot tell you what slowing a real signal would cost in edge. The homework tries it and should be read with that limit attached.

Costs measured in a backtest are themselves estimates. The cost per trade fed into a backtest is a number someone chose, usually from past conditions and past sizes. One view treats it as a parameter to report with an error bar, and asks for the net figure at several costs, as the ladder does. Another warns that a cost chosen after seeing which cost makes the strategy work is one more selection on the same history, the problem sessions 3 and 4 measured, wearing different clothes. Both agree a gross figure should not be read alone. How to set the cost honestly is not settled here.

Part 3 · build-along

Charge a cost by hand, count the flips, run the ladder

18 min hands on

Open this page's console. The engine is loaded as QT. The steps do what Marlow Street's researcher should have done before presenting a gross number: take one world with a real 1.0, compute the rule's Sharpe ratio gross and net, find the cost on the exact days it was charged, count the units, and then run the ladder at the canon seeds. The first four steps use one world of your choosing, so your numbers will differ from anyone else's and from the canon means; the fifth uses the canon seeds and will match exactly.

Make one world with a real 1.0 and take the planted rule

Pick any seed you like. The planted rule is the world's own hidden signal, the one rule with real alpha. One world is one draw, so your numbers will differ.

consolevar w = QT.makeWorld({ trueSR: 1, seed: 7 }); var x = w.signal; w.trueSR, w.phi, x.length

Compute its Sharpe ratio gross and net

The same rule on the same world, once with no cost and once at 5 basis points per unit of position change, over all 755 observations. Expect the net figure to sit roughly a third of a point below the gross one, though on a single world your numbers will differ from the ladder's.

consolevar g = QT.pnl(w, x, { h: 1, costBp: 0 }); var n = QT.pnl(w, x, { h: 1, costBp: 5 }); [QT.annualise(QT.moments(g).sr, 1).toFixed(2), QT.annualise(QT.moments(n).sr, 1).toFixed(2)]

Find the cost on the days it was charged

Subtract the two series observation by observation and express the difference in basis points. These are not statistics; they are the mechanism, and they are the same on every world: the first entry is 5.0, the opening; a hold prints 0.0; a flip prints 10.0, two units at 5 basis points.

consoleg.slice(0, 20).map(function (v, i) { return ((v - n[i]) * 10000).toFixed(1); })

Count the units and compare with 0.41

Turn the signal into positions, add up the absolute changes from one observation to the next, and divide by the number of steps. Then print the arithmetic beside it. On one world your turnover will differ from 0.41; across many worlds it settles there.

consolevar pos = x.slice(0, 755).map(function (s) { return s >= 0 ? 1 : -1; }); var units = 0; for (var i = 1; i < pos.length; i++) units += Math.abs(pos[i] - pos[i - 1]); [(units / (pos.length - 1)).toFixed(3), (2 * Math.acos(0.8) / Math.PI).toFixed(3)]

Run the ladder at the canon seeds

ladderRung with N of 1 and a true Sharpe of 1.0 runs the planted rule alone. Ladder C's first seed is 70,000 plus the cost, so each rung uses its own 200 worlds. It prints 1.01 and 1.05 at 0, 0.69 and 0.74 at 5, and -0.30 and -0.23 at 20. Your numbers will match for the same seed. All three rungs together take well under a second.

console[0, 5, 20].map(function (c) { var r = QT.ladderRung({ N: 1, trueSR: 1, method: "walkforward", costBp: c }, 200, 70000 + c); return [c, r.isSR.toFixed(2), r.oosSR.toFixed(2)]; })

Self-study: costs inside a selection

Every rung above has one rule and no competitors. Put the same real 1.0 among 100 random rules, as session 6 did, and charge a cost. No canon rung exists for this combination, so there are no figures to match and none are quoted here; use 50 worlds at a seed0 of your own, and your numbers will differ. Before you run it, write down what you expect: every rule has the same phi and so roughly the same turnover, so does the cost shift every rule down together, or does it change which rule wins and how often the planted one passes the deflated test? Then read pickedPlant, deflatedDiscovery and oosSR at 0 and at 5. The generator has no memory, so any random rule that wins earns about zero out of sample before costs, not less than zero; after costs it pays the bill on top.

console[0, 5].map(function (c) { var r = QT.ladderRung({ N: 100, trueSR: 1, method: "walkforward", costBp: c }, 50, 424200); return [c, r.pickedPlant.toFixed(2), r.deflatedDiscovery.toFixed(2), r.isSR.toFixed(2), r.oosSR.toFixed(2)]; })

The habit to keep. Never read a Sharpe ratio without asking two things: gross or net, and at what turnover? Then do the arithmetic in your head: turnover times the cost per unit, over the daily volatility, times the square root of 252. If that number is a large share of the edge, the edge is a cost assumption.

Check yourself

Three questions

Starting flat, a rule's positions over five days are +1, +1, -1, -1, +1. How many units of position change does the engine charge?

A · 3: one for the opening and one for each change of sign
B · 5: one unit to open from flat, then two for each of the two flips
C · 2: only the two flips; the opening is free
The engine charges cost times |pos - prev| on every observation, and prev starts at 0. Opening is |+1 - 0| = 1; each flip is |-1 - (+1)| or |+1 - (-1)|, which is 2; holds are 0. One plus two plus two is five.

Why does a real Sharpe ratio of 1.0 lose about 0.32 for every 5 basis points of cost on this world?

A · Because the cost raises the volatility of the strategy's returns
B · Because the cost is charged on every day the position is held
C · Because turnover is about 0.41 units a day, so 5 basis points takes 0.41 x 0.0005 a day, which over a daily standard deviation of 0.01 and times the square root of 252 is about a third of a Sharpe point
The cost comes out of the mean on the days the position changes, and the signal's persistence fixes how often that is: arccos(0.8)/pi = 0.205 flips a day, two units each. The ladder confirms it: 1.01 gross, 0.69 at 5 basis points, averaged over 200 worlds. A hold is never charged, and the cost is far too small to move the standard deviation.

A colleague proposes slowing the signal so that it flips half as often. What is the honest reading?

A · It halves the cost per year at the same cost per trade, so the same edge survives twice the cost; whether a slower signal keeps its edge is an open question that this generator cannot answer, because its edge does not depend on how fast the signal moves
B · It is always better, because lower turnover always raises the net Sharpe ratio
C · It makes no difference, because the cost per trade has not changed
The arithmetic is certain: the cost per year scales with turnover. The trade-off is not: a slower signal trades less but may also say less, and on this bench the dial that sets the edge ignores phi, so the bench shows only the cheaper half of the bargain. That is a limit stated, not a verdict.

Homework

About forty minutes, in the console or in Python.

Find the break-even yourself

The arithmetic puts a real 1.0 at zero near 15 basis points, and the ladder brackets it between 10 and 20. Run the planted rule alone at 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 and 18 basis points over 200 worlds at a seed0 of your own. Your numbers will differ from any canon figure; find the first cost at which the out-of-sample mean turns negative, and check it against the edge divided by about 0.065 per basis point.

Turn the turnover lever

ladderRung passes phi through to the world. Before running anything, compute 2 times arccos(0.95) over pi and predict what 5 basis points should cost a real 1.0 at that turnover. Then run the planted rule alone with phi: 0.95 at 0 and 5 basis points over 200 worlds at a seed0 of your own. Your numbers will differ; compare the drop with your prediction, and write one sentence on why this bench cannot tell you whether a real signal, slowed, would keep its edge.

Write the net line for one backtest you have seen

Take any backtest you have been shown, or one of your own. Write down its turnover in units a day, the cost per unit it assumed, and the net figure beside the gross one. If any of the three is missing, write the question you would ask to get it. Session 8 plants two faults in the data itself, and session 10's brief has a line for exactly this.

Cheat sheet

The one sentence. A gross Sharpe ratio is half a sentence; the other half is turnover times the cost of a unit of trading.
The line. y[t] = pos * fwd - cost * |pos - prev|. Holds pay nothing; a flip pays twice.
The turnover. Sign changes on arccos(phi)/pi of days: 0.205 at phi 0.8, so about 0.41 units a day.
The arithmetic. Turnover x cost, over the daily sd, times sqrt(252). At 5 basis points: about 0.32.
The ladder. A real 1.0 alone, 200 worlds: 1.01 gross, 0.69 at 5, 0.37 at 10, -0.30 at 20.
The break-even. Edge over the cost of one basis point in Sharpe terms. A real 1.0 is gone by about 15.
The two levers. A bigger edge or fewer flips. Selection and a longer history do not make a rule cheaper to trade.
The limit. One cost number per trade, the same at every size. No execution, no microstructure, no capacity figure.
