The deflated Sharpe haircut

Session 3 measured what the best of N rules reports on nothing. Tonight turns that measurement into a test. The ordinary test asks whether a Sharpe ratio beats zero; the deflated test asks whether it beats the best that N tries on nothing would have produced anyway. The change is one argument to one function, and it takes every false pass on the null to zero. It also takes most of the power away from a real rule, and the second half of tonight measures exactly how much. The data is synthetic throughout, and nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Marlow Street wants a test, not a feeling

Marlow Street is a constructed research desk, four people and a head who has been handed a backtest with an annualised Sharpe ratio of 1.8 and asked whether to fund it. After session 3 the desk knows the uncomfortable part: 1.80 is exactly what the best of 100 random rules reports on two years of nothing. Knowing that is not yet a decision rule. Tonight builds the rule, the deflated Sharpe ratio, from its two parts, and then measures what it costs when something real is in the pile.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A probabilistic Sharpe ratio you can read term by term: estimate, sample length, skewness, kurtosis and a benchmark. SR*, the expected best of N null trials, written from equation 1 of Bailey and Lopez de Prado (2014) and checked against the engine. The deflated test, which is the first with the benchmark moved to the second. Two measured facts that belong together: on nothing, false passes fall from 99.5 percent to 0 of 200 worlds at N = 100; with one real 1.5 among 100 on three years of data, the test almost never passes it. And the one cure the numbers allow, which is more independent history.

LiveWhy a haircut, and why it is a test rather than a discount4 min▶

A haircut in this sense is not "take 30 percent off the Sharpe ratio and carry on". A flat discount would treat a rule tried once and a rule kept from a thousand tries the same way, which is the exact mistake session 3 measured. The deflated test changes the question instead: the benchmark the estimate has to beat is no longer zero but SR*, the size of the best estimate that N tries would have produced if nothing were real.

That makes the haircut grow with the search. Tried once, there is nothing to deflate and the test is the ordinary one. Tried ten times on two years, the bar moves to 1.07; a hundred times, 1.80; a thousand times, 2.31. Those are the SR* column of the best-of-N ladder, means over 200 generated worlds each.

Real world

Marlow Street's head, constructed like the rest of the desk, asked the researcher behind the 1.8 one question before any other: how many rules were tried before this one was kept? The answer was "about a hundred, give or take the ones we dropped early". The head wrote 1.80 next to 1.8 on the whiteboard and said the meeting was now about whether the rule beat the first number, not the second.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • PSR. The probabilistic Sharpe ratio: the probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds a benchmark, given the estimate, the number of observations, and their skewness and kurtosis. The engine's QT.psr(sr, n, skew, kurt, benchmark).
  • Benchmark. The last argument of PSR. Zero gives the ordinary test. SR* gives the deflated one.
  • SR*. The expected maximum Sharpe ratio of N independent trials whose true Sharpe ratio is zero, from equation 1 with the variance of the N estimates measured in the trial.
  • V. That variance: how widely the N estimates spread in this world. One estimate on 503 days has a standard deviation of 0.71 annualised by formula, 0.70 measured over 200 worlds.
  • DSR. The deflated Sharpe ratio: PSR with SR* as the benchmark. A rule passes when it reaches 0.95.
  • Naive pass. PSR against zero reaches 0.95. On zero alpha every naive pass is a false one.
  • Power. The share of worlds in which a real rule is kept and passes. The thing the haircut spends.
  • FDR after deflation. The false discovery rate: of the worlds where the deflated test passed, the share where the rule that passed was not the real one.

Part 1 · covers PSR and SR*

Two functions, and the second one moves the first one's benchmark

12 min live

The deflated Sharpe ratio is not a new statistic. It is the probabilistic Sharpe ratio, which already existed, with its benchmark argument filled by the expected maximum of N null trials instead of zero. Bailey and Lopez de Prado put it in one line: DSR is "a PSR where the rejection threshold is adjusted to reflect the multiplicity of trials". Build the two parts and the whole thing is built.

LivePSR: the ordinary test, written so the benchmark can move4 min▶

Session 1 used the probabilistic Sharpe ratio with a benchmark of zero and called it the ordinary test. Here is what is inside it. Take the per-observation estimate sr, subtract the benchmark, multiply by the square root of the number of observations minus one, divide by a correction for the shape of the returns, and read the result off the standard normal curve:

PSR = Phi( (sr - benchmark) * sqrt(n - 1) / sqrt(1 - skew * sr + (kurt - 1) / 4 * sr^2) )

Three things to notice. The numerator is Sharpe 1994's point that a t-statistic is the Sharpe ratio times the square root of T: more observations, sharper verdict. The denominator widens when the returns are skewed against the estimate or fat-tailed, because a ratio computed from such returns is less trustworthy; with normal returns the kurtosis is 3 and the bracket is just 1 plus half of sr squared. And the benchmark is an argument. Nothing in the formula insists it is zero.

On this generator the skew and kurtosis barely move, because each observation is a sign times a normal. On a real series they would, and the formula is unchanged. The 2014 paper's abstract names the two things the full correction targets: "Selection bias under multiple testing and non-Normally distributed returns".

LiveSR*: equation 1, read term by term4 min▶

Equation 1 of Bailey and Lopez de Prado (2014) gives the expected maximum of N independent trials whose true Sharpe ratio is zero:

SR* = sqrt(V) * ( (1 - gamma) * Zinv(1 - 1/N) + gamma * Zinv(1 - 1/(N e)) )

V is the variance of the N estimated Sharpe ratios, measured in the trial, so it carries the sample length with it: short history, wide bell, high SR*. gamma is the Euler-Mascheroni constant, about 0.5772. Zinv is the inverse of the standard normal distribution function, and e is Euler's number. The bracket is the paper's approximation to where the largest of N standard normals lands; session 3 checked it against the measured maximum and found it within 0.1 at N = 10 and within 0.04 at N = 100 and 1,000, in units of one estimate's standard deviation. The paper's own Python snippet, getExpMaxSR, computes the same expression, and so does QT.expectedMaxSR(V, N).

Annualised on 503 days of zero alpha, the best-of-N ladder (200 worlds per rung) prints SR* of 1.07 at N = 10, 1.80 at N = 100 and 2.31 at N = 1,000. At N = 1 there is nothing to be the maximum of, and SR* is zero.

Real world

Marlow Street's quant, constructed, wanted to plug in the 0.71 ruler for the square root of V and skip measuring it. The head asked what would happen on a five-year history or with five-day holds. The ruler would be wrong in both, and the threshold with it. Measuring V from the trials themselves is what lets the same line of code work on any sample length, which is why the engine does it and why you will too in Part 3.

LivePut them together: the deflated pass rate on nothing2 min▶

The deflated test is PSR with SR* as the benchmark, passing at 0.95. The paper describes it as using five variables beyond the estimate: skewness, kurtosis, sample length, the variance of the Sharpe ratios tested, and the number of independent trials. Run on the zero-alpha ladder, 200 worlds per rung, the false-pass rates are:

  • N = 1. Naive 6.0 percent, deflated 6.0 percent. No deflation is possible with one trial, and 6.0 is the 5 percent a one-sided 95 percent test promises, within trial noise.
  • N = 10. Naive 32.5 percent, deflated 0.
  • N = 100. Naive 99.5 percent, deflated 0.
  • N = 1,000. Naive 100 percent, deflated 0.

That is the promise kept. Every one of those naive passes was a false discovery, and the deflated test refused all of them. Part 2 asks what the same refusal does to a rule that deserved to pass.

Self-studyCounting N honestly: independent trials, not attempts3 min▶

Session 3 left this open. Equation 1 is written for N independent trials, and the paper lists "the number of independent trials" among its inputs. On this bench the N rules are independent random signals, so the count is exact. In practice the variants a researcher tries are not independent: a hundred settings of one threshold on one lookback behave like far fewer than a hundred separate tries.

That cuts both ways. Counting correlated variants as independent overstates N and makes the haircut too large. Counting only the variants that "felt different" understates it, and variants dropped early still count: they were tried. There is no single published recipe that settles the effective N for every search, and nothing on this page claims one. The professional habit is to log every variant at the moment it is run, then state the N you used and how you arrived at it beside the deflated figure, so a reader can disagree with the count rather than with a number they cannot audit.

Part 2 · covers the price in power

Plant one real rule among a hundred and see whether the haircut keeps it

12 min live

On nothing the deflated test is perfect. That is the easy half. The hard half is a world where one rule out of 100 really does have an edge, planted through the generator's hidden signal, and the other 99 are noise. Now a pass can be right, and a refusal can be a mistake. Three ladders measure the trade: how strong the real rule is, how big the crowd around it is, and how much history there is to tell them apart.

LiveLadder B: how strong does the real rule have to be?4 min▶

One planted rule among 100, three years of data split 503 training and 252 test observations, 200 worlds per rung. The box above prints any row; the canon is:

  • Real 0.5. Kept in 2.0 percent of worlds, deflated passes 0.
  • Real 1.0. Kept in 14 percent, deflated passes 0.
  • Real 1.5. Kept in 32.5 percent, deflated passes 0.
  • Real 2.0. Kept in 57 percent, deflated passes 3.0 percent.
  • Real 3.0. Kept in 90.5 percent, deflated passes 34.5 percent.

And on every rung the false discovery rate after deflation is 0.000: in each world where the deflated test passed, the rule that passed was the planted one. Where there were no passes the rate is reported as 0.000 because there was nothing to be wrong about, which is a different kind of zero and worth saying out loud.

The naive test, meanwhile, passes in 99 to 100 percent of worlds on every rung, real rule or not. It cannot tell the rungs apart, because the best of 100 clears zero easily whether or not the real one is the best.

Real world

Marlow Street's researcher, constructed, ran the box at a real 1.5 and read "deflated passes 0 percent" as proof that the test is broken. The head's reply was that the test is doing exactly what it promised, refusing everything it cannot distinguish from noise, and that on two years of training data with 99 competitors it cannot distinguish a real 1.5 from noise. The fault, if there is one, is in the amount of history, not in the test.

LiveLadders B2 and B3: the crowd, then the history4 min▶

B2 holds the real rule at 1.5 and grows the crowd (200 worlds each). Alone, N = 1, it passes the test in 65.5 percent of worlds: no deflation, ordinary power. Among 10 the deflated pass rate is 3.0 percent; among 100, 1.5 percent; among 1,000, 1.5 percent. The haircut is almost all spent by the time there are ten rules, and every one of those passes was the planted rule.

B3 holds the crowd at 100 and grows the history (100 worlds each). On 3 years the real 1.5 passes in 3 percent of worlds; on 6 years, 6 percent; on 12, 45 percent; on 24, 94 percent. SR* falls from 1.86 to 0.74 as the bell of estimates narrows, and the real rule is the one kept in 39, then 56, 95 and 100 percent of worlds.

Put the three ladders side by side and the three-year answer for a real 1.5 among 100 is "almost never", not "never": 0 of 200 worlds in ladder B, 1.5 percent of 200 in B2, 3 percent of 100 in B3. Three seed ranges, three small numbers, one conclusion. The 2014 Notices paper reaches the same place from the other direction with its minimum backtest length: the more configurations tried, the more years a backtest needs before its best result means anything.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

One hurdle for everyone. Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) counted 313 papers and 316 factors and concluded that "A new factor needs to clear a much higher hurdle, with a t-statistic greater than 3.0", a p-value of 0.27 percent, adding that there are good reasons to expect 3.0 is too low. The hurdle is calibrated to the whole field's search, and it does not change with what one researcher says they tried.

A hurdle fitted to the search. Bailey and Lopez de Prado (2014) deflate against the expected maximum of the trials actually run, so the bar depends on N, on V and on the sample length. Tried once, the bar is the ordinary test; tried a thousand times, it is far above t of 3.

To see how far apart they can sit, constructed arithmetic on 503 days: a t of 3 is about 3 times the 0.71 ruler, near 2.1 annualised, whatever N was. The deflated bar is SR* plus about 1.17, so about 1.17 at N = 1, about 2.2 at N = 10 and about 3.0 at N = 100. One test is stricter for the honest single try, the other for the wide search.

The DSR paper does not present the two as rivals: it calls the Benjamini-Hochberg style threshold and the DSR threshold complementary and encourages computing both. Harvey, Liu and Zhu would ask who audits a researcher's N; Bailey and Lopez de Prado would ask why a single honest test should pay for the field's search. Both questions are fair. The professional norm is to report both figures beside the N you used, and let the reader see where they disagree.

Self-studyWhat the kept rule earns afterwards, and the limit of this bench3 min▶

The same ladder B rows also record what the kept rule earned on the 252 test days. With nothing real (the zero-alpha rung at N = 100) it is -0.06; with a real 1.5 in the pile it is 0.46, because the real rule is kept a third of the time and a random one the rest; with a real 3.0 it is 2.71. The out-of-sample figure is the honest one, and it is what the deflated test is trying to predict without spending the test days.

The generator has no memory, so a rule kept by luck earns about zero afterwards here, not less than zero. The 2014 Notices paper shows that with memory in the series, overfitting can do worse, through "a compensation effect" that favours a rule in-sample only for it to underperform afterwards. That case is real and this bench cannot show it. Read every out-of-sample zero on this page as "earns nothing, or worse if the series has memory".

Part 3 · build-along

Write SR* yourself, deflate one world, then run the canon row

18 min hands on

Open this page's console. The engine is loaded as QT. Six steps: write equation 1 from scratch and check it against the engine, build one world with a real 1.5 among 100, measure V and SR* in it, ask both tests about the kept rule, confirm the engine agrees, then let the engine repeat the whole thing over 200 worlds. One world's figures are one draw: compare them with the engine, never quote them as a result.

Write equation 1 yourself

Use only the inverse normal and the Euler-Mascheroni constant the engine exposes. With V = 1 the result is in units of one estimate's standard deviation, so you can hold it against session 3's equation 1 column: 1.575 at N = 10, 2.531 at N = 100, 3.255 at N = 1,000. Yours and the engine's should agree to every digit, since no random number is involved.

consolefunction mySRStar(V, N) { var g = QT.EULER_GAMMA; return Math.sqrt(V) * ((1 - g) * QT.normInv(1 - 1 / N) + g * QT.normInv(1 - 1 / (N * Math.E))); } [mySRStar(1, 10), mySRStar(1, 100), mySRStar(1, 1000)].map(function (v) { return v.toFixed(3); }) [QT.expectedMaxSR(1, 100).toFixed(3), mySRStar(1, 100).toFixed(3)]

Build one world with a real 1.5 among 100

Seed 50016 is the first world of ladder B's real-1.5 rung. The planted rule sits at index 0 and is given the world's hidden signal; the other 99 are random. Score each on the first 503 observations. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

consolevar w = QT.makeWorld({ trueSR: 1.5, seed: 50016 }); var xs = QT.makeStrategies(w, { N: 100, plantIndex: 0, seed: w.seed + 1000 }); var ys = xs.map(function (x) { return QT.pnl(w, x, { h: 1 }); }); var srs = ys.map(function (y) { return QT.moments(y.slice(0, 503)).sr; }); srs.length

Measure V and SR* in this world

V is the variance of the 100 per-observation estimates, measured, not assumed. Feed it to your function and annualise. This is one world's bar; the canon figures are means over 200 worlds. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

consolevar V = Math.pow(QT.moments(srs).sd, 2); var srStar = mySRStar(V, 100); [V.toExponential(3), QT.annualise(srStar, 1).toFixed(2)]

Keep the best, then ask both questions

Find the rule with the highest in-sample estimate, note whether it is index 0 (the real one), and compute its PSR twice: against zero, the naive test, and against SR*, the deflated one. Watch how far the same estimate moves when only the benchmark changes. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

consolevar best = srs.indexOf(Math.max.apply(null, srs)); var m = QT.moments(ys[best].slice(0, 503)); [best, QT.annualise(m.sr, 1).toFixed(2), QT.psr(m.sr, m.n, m.skew, m.kurt, 0).toFixed(3), QT.psr(m.sr, m.n, m.skew, m.kurt, srStar).toFixed(3)]

Check your hand-built world against the engine

QT.trial runs the same world with the same seeds and reports the kept rule's annualised in-sample Sharpe ratio and SR*. Both should equal what you computed in steps 3 and 4. If they do not, the difference is in your code, and finding it is the exercise. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

consolevar t = QT.trial({ N: 100, trueSR: 1.5, method: "walkforward", seed: 50016 }); [t.isSR.toFixed(2), t.srStar.toFixed(2), t.pickedPlant, t.naiveDiscovery, t.deflatedDiscovery]

Run the canon row: 200 worlds

ladderRung repeats the trial over seeds 50016 to 50215 and averages. This is 200 worlds of 100 rules, 20,000 strategies scored, so allow a second or two before it prints; the tab is busy, not stuck. It prints ladder B's real-1.5 row: kept 0.325, naive 0.995, deflated 0, false discovery rate after deflation 0. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

consolevar r = QT.ladderRung({ N: 100, trueSR: 1.5, method: "walkforward" }, 200, 50015); [r.isSR.toFixed(2), r.oosSR.toFixed(2), r.pickedPlant.toFixed(3), r.naiveDiscovery.toFixed(3), r.deflatedDiscovery.toFixed(3), r.fdrAfterDeflation.toFixed(3)]

The habit to keep. Never report a Sharpe ratio, or a pass, without the N it was the best of and the SR* that N implies. If the N is unknown, the deflated figure cannot be computed, and that is the finding to report.

Check yourself

Three questions

What does deflation change inside the probabilistic Sharpe ratio?

A · It multiplies the estimated Sharpe ratio by a fixed discount that grows with N
B · Only the benchmark: zero is replaced by SR*, the expected maximum of N null trials computed from the measured variance of the N estimates
C · It replaces the skewness and kurtosis terms with the number of trials
DSR is "a PSR where the rejection threshold is adjusted to reflect the multiplicity of trials". The estimate, the sample length and the shape terms stay; the benchmark moves from 0 to SR*, which is 1.07, 1.80 and 2.31 at N = 10, 100 and 1,000 on 503 days (200 worlds per rung).

With one real 1.5 among 100 rules on three years of data, the deflated test passed in 0 of 200 worlds in ladder B. What is the right reading?

A · The rule has no edge, since it never passes
B · The test is broken, since it refused a real rule
C · A power problem: the real rule is kept in only 32.5 percent of worlds and almost never clears SR* on this much history; on 24 years the same rule passes in 94 percent of 100 worlds
"Almost never", not "never": 0 of 200 in ladder B, 1.5 percent in B2 at N = 100, 3 percent of 100 in B3. The test keeps its promise on false passes (0.000 false discovery rate after deflation) and the only cure the bench shows is more independent history.

Why does the engine measure V, the variance of the N estimates, inside each trial instead of plugging in the 0.71 ruler?

A · Because V carries the sample length and the hold with it, so the same code gives the right SR* on 3 years or 24, where SR* falls from 1.86 to 0.74
B · Because 0.71 is only an approximation and measuring is always more accurate
C · Because equation 1 does not work with a constant variance
0.71 is the ruler for 503 daily observations only. Equation 1 is written in terms of the variance of the trials' estimated Sharpe ratios, and measuring it lets one line of code follow the history: ladder B3 prints SR* of 1.86, 1.35, 0.99 and 0.74 for 3, 6, 12 and 24 years (100 worlds each).

Take this with you

Cheat sheet

The one sentence. Deflation moves the benchmark from zero to the best that N tries on nothing would have produced, and tests against that.
PSR. Phi((sr - benchmark) times sqrt(n - 1), over sqrt(1 - skew sr + (kurt - 1)/4 sr squared)). Pass at 0.95.
SR*. sqrt(V) times ((1 - gamma) Zinv(1 - 1/N) + gamma Zinv(1 - 1/(N e))), gamma 0.5772, V measured from the N estimates.
The bar. On 503 days: SR* 1.07 at N = 10, 1.80 at 100, 2.31 at 1,000. At N = 1 there is nothing to deflate.
The promise. On nothing, false passes go from 99.5 percent naive to 0 deflated at N = 100 (200 worlds). FDR after deflation 0.000 on every planted rung.
The price. A real 1.5 among 100 on three years almost never passes: 0 of 200 in B, 1.5 percent in B2, 3 percent of 100 in B3.
The cure. Independent history. The same real 1.5 passes 45 percent on 12 years and 94 percent on 24 as SR* falls to 0.74.
The report. Every Sharpe ratio comes with its N, its SR*, its sample length, and the t above 3 verdict beside the deflated one.
