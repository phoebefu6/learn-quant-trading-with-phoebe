Generating the honest null

Every claim in the next nine sessions is held against one object: a series with nothing in it. Not a quiet market, not a bad year, nothing. A seeded random walk whose alpha is exactly zero because the generator has a dial for alpha and the dial is at zero. Tonight builds that generator, reads its parts, and measures the one number every backtest is secretly compared to: how big a Sharpe ratio pure noise produces on two years of daily data. The data is synthetic throughout, and nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Marlow Street, before the first backtest

Marlow Street is a constructed research desk, four people and a head who funds two strategies a year. Its first good decision, which it reached late, was to build the thing it would compare every backtest to before building any backtest at all. That thing is a generator with the truth as a parameter. When the alpha dial is at zero the series contains nothing, and whatever a method reports on it is, by construction, noise. Tonight is that generator, line by line, and the first measurement taken with it.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. A seeded generator you can read in full, with every random number traceable to a seed and every return traceable to the numbers that made it. The reason the alpha is planted through a hidden signal rather than added as a drift, which matters for every later session. A measured answer to "how big is a Sharpe ratio on nothing": a standard deviation of 0.70 measured over 200 worlds against 0.71 from the formula, annualised on 503 daily observations, and 6 worlds in 100 clearing a 95 percent test. And the habit of running every method on the null first.

LiveWhy the null has to be built, not assumed4 min▶

A data scientist checking a classifier has a confusion matrix and a holdout set. A researcher checking a backtest has one path of history and no second one. The only way to know what a method reports when there is nothing to find is to make a world where there is nothing to find and run the method on it. That is not a simulation of a market. It is a calibration of the method.

Synthetic data is a deliberate choice. A real series would leave the truth unknown, so every figure would be an opinion. A generated series has its alpha as a parameter, so every figure is a measurement of the method against a known answer. The cost is that no number here describes any market, and the pages say so wherever it could be mistaken.

Real world

Marlow Street's second researcher, constructed like the rest, brought a rule with a Sharpe ratio of 0.9 and called it modest but real. The head asked what the same rule reported on the null. Nobody had run it. When they did, across 200 generated worlds, a rule with no information reported above 0.9 in 17 of them. The rule was not wrong. It was unread, because the ruler had never been built.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Seed. One integer that fixes every random number the generator will ever produce. Same seed, same world, in node, in the browser and in the Python reference.
  • Random walk. A series whose increments are independent draws with no exploitable structure. Here: daily returns of volatility one percent.
  • Hidden signal. An AR(1) series the returns lean on when alpha is planted. With alpha at zero its weight is exactly zero and the returns are pure noise.
  • Alpha. Here, the annualised Sharpe ratio a strategy trading the hidden signal's sign would earn in expectation. A dial on the generator, nothing more.
  • Position. Plus one or minus one: long or short one unit. Session 2 turns a signal into one.
  • Sharpe ratio. Mean over standard deviation of the strategy's observations, annualised by the square root of 252. Session 2 builds it from scratch; tonight only measures its noise.
  • Trial. One generated world and whatever was run on it. Every number on every page is a mean over a stated number of trials.
  • Walk-forward. Train on the first two thirds in time order, test on the last third. Session 5 does it properly; tonight only uses it.

Part 1 · covers the generator

From one seed to a series with nothing in it

12 min live

The generator is four stages and none of them is clever. A seed feeds a small integer mixer that produces uniform numbers. Pairs of uniforms become standard normals. One stream of normals becomes a hidden AR(1) signal; a second stream becomes the returns, which lean on yesterday's signal by a weight that is zero tonight. The whole thing is about forty lines, and every one of them can be read.

LiveThe four stages, read as code5 min▶

Seed to uniforms. mulberry32 is a 32-bit integer mixer: add a constant, multiply and shift a few times, divide by 2 to the 32. It is not cryptographic and does not need to be. What it needs to be is identical in node, in the browser and in Python, so that a seed names a world and the Python reference reproduces every number. It is.

Uniforms to normals. Box-Muller takes two uniforms and returns the square root of minus two times the log of the first, times the cosine of two pi times the second. One normal per pair, no caching, so the stream is the same everywhere.

Normals to a signal. The signal is an AR(1): each day is 0.8 times yesterday plus a scaled fresh normal. The 0.8 is there so that a position built from it persists for a few days, which is what makes costs and overlapping observations meaningful later. It is not a claim about any market.

Normals to returns. Each day's return is one percent times a fresh normal, plus a weight c times yesterday's signal, the whole thing rescaled so volatility stays at one percent. The weight is chosen so that a strategy trading the signal's sign earns the requested annualised Sharpe ratio in expectation. Tonight the request is zero, so c is zero, and the returns are noise.

LiveWhy alpha is planted through a signal, not a drift4 min▶

The easy way to plant alpha is to add a positive drift to the returns. The problem is that a drift rewards buy-and-hold, which has no signal in it and no selection problem. Every later session is about a rule that reads something and takes a position, and the only way to make a rule genuinely right is to give the world something it could read.

So the generator hides a signal and lets the returns lean on yesterday's value of it. A rule that trades the sign of that signal is right more often than not, by exactly the amount c controls. A rule that trades any other signal is a coin flip. That is the whole difference between a real edge and a selected one, and it is why the generator can later plant one real rule among a thousand random ones and ask whether the correction keeps it.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree about synthetic worlds4 min▶

One camp holds that a generator this simple teaches nothing about markets, because real series have fat tails, clustered volatility, regimes and memory, and every method's failure mode depends on those. On this view the honest null is the real series with its labels shuffled, not a normal random walk.

The other camp holds that the methods on trial here, selection, deflation, splits, costs, fail on a plain random walk already, and a method that fails on the simplest world does not need a harder one to be convicted. On this view the generator is a floor: anything a method gets wrong here it will also get wrong in a world with tails and memory, and usually worse.

Both are right about different questions. The measurements here take the second position and says so: the generator has no memory, so the AMS paper's warning that memory makes overfit rules lose rather than merely earn nothing is a case the bench cannot show. That is a limit, stated, not a resolution.

Part 2 · covers the noise in one estimate

How big a Sharpe ratio nothing produces

12 min live

Take one random rule, run it on one zero-alpha world for 503 days, compute its annualised Sharpe ratio, and repeat for 200 worlds. The spread of those 200 numbers is the ruler every later number is measured with. It is wider than most people guess, and the width is a property of the sample length, not of the rule.

LiveReading the box4 min▶

The top rows are one world: the rule's in-sample Sharpe ratio on 503 days, its out-of-sample figure on the next 252, and the probability, under the ordinary one-sided test, that the true ratio is above zero. Change the world and watch all three move, because the rule knows nothing and the world is noise.

The bottom rows are the measurement that matters: over 200 worlds the mean in-sample ratio is -0.01, the standard deviation of the estimates is 0.70 (the formula says 0.71), and 6.0 percent of worlds clear the 95 percent threshold. That last figure is the false-positive rate of the ordinary test when it is applied once, and it is about what a 95 percent test should give. Session 3 shows what happens to it when the test is applied to the best of many.

LiveWhy 0.71, and why it is the number to memorise3 min▶

A per-day Sharpe estimate on T independent observations has a standard deviation of roughly one over the square root of T. On 503 days that is 0.045 per day, which annualised by the square root of 252 is 0.71. The 0.71 is a fact about 503 days, not about the rule or the generator. Any rule, any world of this length, same width.

Every later number sits on this ruler. A best-of-100 figure of 1.80 is two and a half rulers out. A deflation threshold of 1.80 is the same thing said the other way round. A real edge of 1.5 that gets selected only a third of the time is a 1.5 competing against a hundred draws from a bell this wide. When a backtest number arrives without a sample length, you cannot know how wide its bell is, and that is the first thing to ask.

Self-studyThe probabilistic Sharpe ratio, named tonight and built in session 43 min▶

The box's third row is a probabilistic Sharpe ratio: the probability that the true ratio exceeds a benchmark, given the estimate, the sample length and the skewness and kurtosis of the observations. Bailey and Lopez de Prado developed it in 2012 and used it in 2014 as the inside of the deflated Sharpe ratio. Tonight it is used with a benchmark of zero, which is the ordinary test. Session 4 replaces the zero with the expected maximum of N trials, which is the whole correction.

The skewness and kurtosis terms barely move on this generator, because the observations are products of a sign and a normal. On a real series they matter, and the formula is the same. What changes is only what you put into it.

Part 3 · build-along

Make a world, read it, measure its noise

18 min hands on

Open this page's console. The engine is already loaded as QT, and everything the box above did can be done by hand in six calls. Your numbers will match the box exactly for the same seed, because the seed is the world.

Make one world with nothing in it

Seed 10001 is World 1 in the box above. The world carries its returns, its hidden signal (unused, since trueSR is 0) and its seed.

consolevar w = QT.makeWorld({ trueSR: 0, seed: 10001 }); w.days, w.sigma, w.returns.slice(0, 5)

Make one random rule and turn it into observations

The strategy seed is the world seed plus 1000, which is the convention every page uses. The pnl is position times the next day's return, one observation per day, 755 of them on a 756-day world.

consolevar x = QT.makeStrategies(w, { N: 1, seed: w.seed + 1000 })[0]; var y = QT.pnl(w, x, { h: 1 }); y.length

Score the first 503 days and the next 252 separately

moments returns the mean, the sample standard deviation and their ratio per observation; annualise multiplies by the square root of 252. Your two numbers are the first two rows of the box for World 1.

consolevar mIS = QT.moments(y.slice(0, 503)), mOOS = QT.moments(y.slice(503)); [QT.annualise(mIS.sr, 1).toFixed(2), QT.annualise(mOOS.sr, 1).toFixed(2)]

Ask the ordinary question: is it above zero?

The probabilistic Sharpe ratio with a benchmark of zero. Above 0.95 is the usual declaration of a finding. On this world it is whatever the noise made it.

consoleQT.psr(mIS.sr, mIS.n, mIS.skew, mIS.kurt, 0).toFixed(3)

Now do it 200 times and read the spread

ladderRung runs the same six calls over 200 seeds starting at seed0 plus 1 and reports means. The canon for this rung uses seed0 of 10000, so the mean in-sample figure prints -0.01 and the naive discovery rate prints 0.060. Your numbers will match, not approximately: exactly.

consolevar r = QT.ladderRung({ N: 1, trueSR: 0, method: "walkforward" }, 200, 10000); [r.isSR.toFixed(2), r.oosSR.toFixed(2), r.naiveDiscovery.toFixed(3)]

Measure the width yourself

ladderRung reports means, not spreads, so collect the 200 in-sample figures by hand and take their standard deviation. It prints 0.70 at the canon seeds, beside the formula's 0.71, which is the ruler for every later session.

consolevar v = []; for (var i = 1; i <= 200; i++) { var t = QT.trial({ N: 1, trueSR: 0, method: "walkforward", seed: 10000 + i }); v.push(t.isSR); } QT.moments(v).sd.toFixed(2)

The habit to keep. Before any method is allowed to say it found something, run it on the null and write down what it reports there. If the method cannot be run on a null, that is the finding, and it is a serious one.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why does the generator plant alpha through a hidden signal the returns lean on, rather than by adding a drift to the returns?

Every later session is about rules that read something and pick a side. A drift has no rule to find. A hidden signal with a controllable weight gives the world one true rule and lets later sessions ask whether a correction keeps it among a thousand false ones.

One random rule on 503 days of a zero-alpha series: what is the standard deviation of its annualised Sharpe estimate across worlds, and where does the number come from?

The true ratio is zero; the estimate is not. Its width depends only on how many observations went into it, which is why a backtest without a sample length cannot be read. The volatility cancels out of the ratio entirely.

The box reports that 6.0 percent of 200 zero-alpha worlds clear the 95 percent threshold with one rule. What does that number mean?

A 95 percent one-sided test passes about one null world in twenty by design. Six in a hundred at 200 trials is that figure within noise. Session 3 applies the same test to the best of 100 rules and the pass rate becomes 99.5 percent, which is the whole problem.

Cheat sheet

The one sentence. Build the world where nothing is there before you test anything, and write down what the method reports on it.
The seed. One integer names a world in node, the browser and Python. World seed s, strategies s + 1000, shuffles s + 2000.
The dial. trueSR sets the weight c on a hidden AR(1) signal. At zero the returns are one percent times noise.
The ruler. 0.71 annualised: the standard deviation of a Sharpe estimate on 503 days with nothing there.
The baseline. 6 of 100 zero-alpha worlds pass a 95 percent test with one rule. That is the test working.
The arithmetic. t equals Sharpe times the square root of T (Sharpe 1994). On 503 days, 1.17 annualised is t of 1.65.
The limit. No memory in the generator, so overfit rules earn zero here, not less than zero. Say so.
The loop. One world is one number. The spread across worlds is the only output that means anything.
