The max of N

Session 1 measured the noise in one estimate. Session 2 built the estimate. Tonight asks the question nobody puts on the slide: what was it the best of? Try N rules on a world with nothing in it, keep the one with the highest Sharpe ratio, and measure what it reports and what it earns on days it never saw. Then measure how fast that maximum grows with N, and hold the measurement against the published formula and the published bound. The data is synthetic throughout, and nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Marlow Street's 1.8, and what it was the best of

Marlow Street is a constructed research desk, four people and a head who funds two strategies a year. The head has been handed a backtest with an annualised Sharpe ratio of 1.8 and asked whether to fund it. Session 1 said a single estimate on two years of nothing has a standard deviation of 0.71, so 1.8 is about two and a half of those out. That would be a strong reading for one rule. It is an ordinary reading for the best of many, and tonight measures exactly how ordinary.

★ What you walk out with tonight. The best-of-N ladder measured on pure noise at 200 worlds per rung: best in-sample Sharpe ratios of 1.04, 1.80 and 2.33 for 10, 100 and 1,000 rules, and out-of-sample figures of 0.14, -0.06 and 0.02 for the same kept rules. The reason those out-of-sample figures sit at zero rather than below it on this generator. Equation 1 of the deflated Sharpe paper, read term by term, and the sqrt(2 ln N) bound, each held against the measured maximum. And the habit of asking, before reading any backtest, how many things were tried to get it.

LiveWhy the best of N is a different statistic4 min▶

One rule's Sharpe ratio on a zero-alpha world is a draw from a bell centred on zero with a standard deviation of 0.71 on 503 days. The best of N rules is not a draw from that bell. It is the largest of N draws, and the largest of N draws has its own distribution, sitting further right the more draws there are. Reading the maximum as if it were one draw is the whole error, and it needs no fraud and no bug. It needs only a loop that tries things and keeps the winner.

The fix starts with a number nobody likes to report: N. With N in hand, the maximum can be compared to what the maximum of N draws on nothing looks like. Without it, the 1.8 cannot be read at all.

Real world

At Marlow Street, constructed like the rest, the head asked the researcher who brought the 1.8 how many variants had been run before it. The answer, after some reconstruction from old notebooks, was about a hundred: lookback lengths, thresholds, holding periods. Nobody had lied. Each variant was a reasonable idea. The question tonight is what the best of a hundred reasonable ideas reports on a world where none of them can work.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • N. The number of rules tried before one was kept. On this bench the N rules are independent random signals; in practice they are variants, and counting them honestly is session 4's problem.
  • Best in-sample. The highest Sharpe ratio among the N, measured on the 503 training days. The number that gets presented.
  • Out of sample. The kept rule's Sharpe ratio on the next 252 days, which played no part in choosing it.
  • The ruler. The standard deviation of one estimate on 503 days: 0.71 annualised. Part 2 measures the maximum in rulers.
  • Equation 1. The expected maximum of N independent null trials in the deflated Sharpe paper, built from the normal quantile function and gamma, about 0.5772.
  • sqrt(2 ln N). An upper bound to that expected maximum, in units of one estimate's standard deviation. A bound, not an estimate.
  • SR*. Equation 1 applied with the measured spread of the N estimates, annualised. Session 4 uses it as the threshold.
  • Naive discovery. The share of worlds where the kept rule's probabilistic Sharpe ratio against zero reaches 0.95. On zero alpha, every one is false.

Part 1 · covers best-of-N on pure noise

Keep the best of N on nothing, then look again

12 min live

The experiment is three lines. Generate a world with no alpha. Generate N random rules and score each on the first 503 days. Keep the highest, and score that one rule again on the next 252 days. Repeat for 200 worlds and average. The first number is what a backtest presents; the second is what the rule actually had. The selection loop runs on the same days it is scored on, which is why the first number tells you about the loop and nothing about the rule.

LiveReading the box, rung by rung4 min▶

N = 1. No selection. The mean in-sample figure is -0.01, out of sample -0.07, and the naive test passes in 6.0 percent of worlds, which is the ordinary false-positive rate of a one-sided 95 percent test within trial noise. Session 1's baseline, reproduced.

N = 10. The kept rule reports 1.04 in-sample and 0.14 out of sample. The naive test now passes in 32.5 percent of worlds. Nothing changed about the rules; only the act of keeping the best one.

N = 100. 1.80 in-sample, -0.06 out of sample, and the naive test passes in 99.5 percent of worlds. This is Marlow Street's number, produced by a loop over a hundred rules that know nothing.

N = 1,000. 2.33 in-sample, 0.02 out of sample, naive passes in every one of the 200 worlds. The in-sample figure is a function of how hard someone looked. The out-of-sample figure is a function of what was there.

The drop from in-sample to out of sample is 0.9 at N = 10, 1.9 at N = 100 and 2.3 at N = 1,000. Every one of those drops is the selection loop's contribution, and none of it is the rule's.

LiveWhat the 2014 Notices paper already measured4 min▶

Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado and Zhu made the same argument in the Notices of the American Mathematical Society in 2014, with three illustrations worth carrying around:

  • Twenty trials. At a 5 percent false-positive rate, after only twenty trials the researcher is expected to find one specification that passes. The test is working; the twenty is the problem.
  • Seven trials, two years. After seven independent configurations, the expected maximum in-sample Sharpe ratio on a two-year backtest is 1, while the expected out-of-sample figure is 0. The bench's 1.04 at N = 10 on 503 training days is the same shape at a slightly larger N.
  • 128 trials. Seven binary choices give 2 to the 7, or 128 combinations, with an expected maximum Sharpe ratio above 2.6. Seven yes-or-no design decisions is an ordinary week.

The paper's figures come from its own setup; the bench's come from this generator. They agree on the mechanism and on the order of magnitude, which is the point of running both.

Real world

Marlow Street's researcher, constructed, objected that the hundred variants were not independent: many shared a lookback and differed only in a threshold. That is a fair objection and it cuts both ways. Correlated variants make the effective N smaller than a hundred, so the maximum is less inflated than the bench's 1.80. But nobody at the desk can say how much smaller without counting, and counting is session 4's work. Until then the head reads the 1.8 against the best of 100 on nothing, which is 1.80.

LiveZero, not negative: the limit of this bench3 min▶

Look again at the out-of-sample column: 0.14, -0.06, 0.02. None of them is reliably below zero. That is not luck and it is not a property of overfitting in general. It is a property of this generator, which has no memory: each day's return is independent of every earlier one, so the rule that happened to fit the first 503 days carries nothing, good or bad, into the next 252.

The AMS paper is explicit about this. Without memory there is no reason to expect overfitting to produce negative performance. With memory there is a compensation effect: the configuration that fits a stretch of history best is the one most likely to be positioned wrongly when that stretch reverses, so it is selected in-sample only to underperform out of sample. The bench cannot show that case. The honest sentence is that an overfit rule earns nothing, or worse if the series has memory, which this bench does not model.

Self-studyWhy the naive test passes 99.5 percent of the time3 min▶

The naive test is a probabilistic Sharpe ratio against a benchmark of zero, declared a finding at 0.95. Applied to one rule it does what it promises: about 5 percent of null worlds pass, and the bench measures 6.0 percent over 200. Applied to the kept rule it is answering a question nobody asked. It asks whether this one estimate is far from zero, when the estimate was chosen precisely for being far from zero.

The fix is not a stricter p-value picked by feel. It is to move the benchmark from zero to what the maximum of N looks like on nothing, which is what Part 2 measures and what session 4 turns into the deflated Sharpe ratio. As a preview, the same 200 worlds per rung pass that deflated test in 0.000 of worlds at N = 10, 100 and 1,000.

Part 2 · covers how the maximum grows

How fast the maximum grows with N

12 min live

Take the best in-sample figures from Part 1 and divide out the ruler, so that each maximum is counted in units of one estimate's standard deviation. Now the numbers no longer depend on the sample length, and they can be held against two published predictions: equation 1 of the deflated Sharpe paper, which is meant to estimate the expected maximum, and sqrt(2 ln N), which the AMS paper gives as an upper bound to it. The measured column goes beside both, every time.

LiveEquation 1, read term by term5 min▶

Bailey and Lopez de Prado (2014) give the expected maximum of N independent null trials as the square root of V times a blend of two normal quantiles: (1 - gamma) times Z-1[1 - 1/N], plus gamma times Z-1[1 - 1/(N e)]. V is the variance of the trials' estimated Sharpe ratios, Z-1 is the inverse of the standard normal distribution function, e is the base of the natural log, and gamma is the Euler-Mascheroni constant, about 0.5772. The paper's own code snippet computes exactly this, and so does QT.expectedMaxSR.

  • Z-1[1 - 1/N]. The level that one draw in N is expected to exceed. For N = 100 it is the 99th percentile of a normal. This is where the maximum lives, roughly.
  • Z-1[1 - 1/(N e)]. The same quantile pushed a little further out. The distance between the two quantiles is the spread of the maximum.
  • The blend. (1 - gamma) a + gamma b is the same as a + gamma (b - a): a location plus gamma times a scale. That is the mean of a Gumbel distribution, mu + beta gamma, which is the limiting distribution of the maximum of many normals (Fisher-Tippett-Gnedenko, read on the Wikipedia pages). Equation 1 is the Gumbel mean with the normal's own location and scale plugged in.
  • sqrt(V). Everything above is in units of one estimate's standard deviation. Multiply by the spread of the estimates and you have a Sharpe ratio. With V = 1 the equation returns rulers, which is how Part 2 reads it.
LiveMeasured against both predictions4 min▶

The bench's maximum, in rulers, is the mean best in-sample per-day Sharpe ratio times the square root of 503. Over 200 worlds per rung:

  • N = 10. Measured 1.475, equation 1 1.575, sqrt(2 ln N) 2.146.
  • N = 100. Measured 2.539, equation 1 2.531, sqrt(2 ln N) 3.035.
  • N = 1,000. Measured 3.291, equation 1 3.255, sqrt(2 ln N) 3.717.

Equation 1 lands within 0.1 of the measurement at N = 10 and within 0.04 at N = 100 and 1,000. The paper's own Exhibit 3.1 note reports the formula overestimating the empirical maximum by less than 0.05 below about 50 trials for a process with variance 1, and by as little as 0.006 by 1,000 trials. The bench's gap at N = 10 is larger than that, and it is a mean of maxima over only 200 worlds whose estimates are not exactly unit-variance normals; treat it as what this bench measured, not as a correction to the paper.

The bound is another matter. It sits well above the measurement on every rung, 2.146 against 1.475 at N = 10 and still 3.717 against 3.291 at N = 1,000. It was never meant to sit on it. The AMS paper offers sqrt(2 ln N) as an upper bound to the expected maximum, and as a bound it holds on every rung.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Read a few papers and talks on backtest overfitting and sqrt(2 ln N) turns up in two different roles. In the AMS paper it is written down as an upper bound to the expected maximum, and its Theorem 2 uses it to bound the minimum backtest length. In looser speech it is the expected maximum, or the rate at which the maximum grows, and people quote it as if it were the number to compare a backtest against.

The case for the loose reading: the normalising constant of the maximum of n normals is approximately sqrt(2 ln n), so as N grows the maximum really does grow like sqrt(2 ln N), and a one-line formula is easier to carry into a meeting than equation 1. The case against: at the N anyone actually tries, the bound overstates the maximum by about half a ruler, 3.035 against a measured 2.539 at N = 100, and a threshold set at the bound kills real findings that equation 1 would let through.

Both are describing the same mathematics at different resolutions. The measurements here take no side on which one a desk should say aloud. They do insist on one thing: when sqrt(2 ln N) is quoted, call it a bound, and put the measured column next to it.

Self-studyFrom rulers back to Sharpe ratios3 min▶

A ruler is 0.71 annualised on 503 daily observations: one over the square root of 503 per day, times the square root of 252. Multiply any figure in rulers by 0.71 and you have the annualised figure Part 1 printed: 1.475 rulers is the 1.04, 2.539 is the 1.80, 3.291 is the 2.33. The ruler shrinks as the history grows, which is why the same N inflates a short backtest more than a long one, and why session 4's ladder with more years is the only cure the haircut has.

The SR* row in the box is equation 1 with V measured from the spread of the N estimates in each world, then annualised: 1.07, 1.80 and 2.31 for N of 10, 100 and 1,000. It tracks the best in-sample figure closely on every rung, which is the point: the expected best of N on nothing is what the best of N on nothing reports.

Real world

The Marlow Street head, constructed, now has a sentence for the funding memo. The backtest reports 1.8 after about a hundred variants on two years of training data. The expected best of a hundred rules on nothing, measured over 200 generated worlds at that length, is 1.80. The backtest is exactly as good as nothing, and the next question is not whether to fund it but how many of the hundred were really independent.

Part 3 · build-along

Measure the maximum in rulers, by hand

18 min hands on

Open this page's console. The engine is already loaded as QT. Six steps take you from the two formulas to the measured ladder and back to annualised Sharpe ratios. The formula steps are arithmetic with no seed in them; the ladder steps use the canon seeds, so your numbers will match for the same seed.

Start with the bound

sqrt(2 ln N) at N = 100. This is arithmetic with no seed in it, so it prints 3.035 in every browser. Keep it in mind as the ceiling, not the estimate.

consoleQT.sqrt2lnN(100).toFixed(3)

Then equation 1, in rulers

expectedMaxSR takes the variance of the trials' estimates and N. With V = 1 it returns the expected maximum in units of one estimate's standard deviation: 1.575, 2.531 and 3.255 for N of 10, 100 and 1,000. Again arithmetic, again the same everywhere. Note that every value sits under the bound from step 1.

console[10, 100, 1000].map(function (n) { return QT.expectedMaxSR(1, n).toFixed(3); })

Run the best of 10 on 200 zero-alpha worlds

The canon seed rule for this ladder is seed0 = 10,000 times N, so N = 10 starts at 100,000. The rung prints the mean best in-sample figure and its out-of-sample figure over 200 worlds: 1.04 and 0.14. Your numbers will match for the same seed, to every digit.

consolevar r = QT.ladderRung({ N: 10, trueSR: 0, method: "walkforward" }, 200, 100000); [r.isSR.toFixed(2), r.oosSR.toFixed(2), r.naiveDiscovery.toFixed(3)]

Put the measurement in rulers and compare

r.isPerObs is the mean best in-sample Sharpe ratio per day. Times the square root of 503 it is in rulers: 1.475 against equation 1's 1.575 and the bound's 2.146. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

console[(r.isPerObs * Math.sqrt(503)).toFixed(3), QT.expectedMaxSR(1, 10).toFixed(3), QT.sqrt2lnN(10).toFixed(3)]

Now the best of 100: Marlow Street's count

Same call with N = 100 and seed0 = 1,000,000. Expect about a second. It prints 1.80 in-sample, -0.06 out of sample, SR* of 1.80, and 2.539 rulers against equation 1's 2.531. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

consolevar r100 = QT.ladderRung({ N: 100, trueSR: 0, method: "walkforward" }, 200, 1000000); [r100.isSR.toFixed(2), r100.oosSR.toFixed(2), r100.srStar.toFixed(2), (r100.isPerObs * Math.sqrt(503)).toFixed(3)]

Turn rulers back into a Sharpe ratio, and decide about N = 1,000

Multiply the rulers by the square root of 252 over 503, the 0.71, and you get the annualised figure back. The N = 1,000 rung with seed0 = 10,000,000 prints 2.33, 0.02 and 3.291 rulers, but at 200 worlds it takes many seconds in a browser, and the tab will look frozen while it runs. Run it when you have the time, not mid-session.

console(r100.isPerObs * Math.sqrt(503) * Math.sqrt(252 / 503)).toFixed(2) // optional, slow: var r1k = QT.ladderRung({ N: 1000, trueSR: 0, method: "walkforward" }, 200, 10000000);

The habit to keep. Never read a Sharpe ratio without the N it was the best of. Divide out the ruler, hold the result against equation 1 for that N, and if the two are close, the backtest has told you how many things were tried and nothing else.

Check yourself

Three questions

At 200 worlds per rung the kept rule's out-of-sample Sharpe ratio is 0.14, -0.06 and 0.02 for N of 10, 100 and 1,000. Why is it about zero here rather than reliably negative?

A · Because the kept rule secretly has a small real edge that cancels the overfitting
B · Because the generator has no memory: each day is independent of the last, so a rule fitted to the first 503 days carries nothing into the next 252, and the AMS paper's compensation effect needs memory to make overfit rules lose
C · Because 200 worlds is too few to see the loss that overfitting always causes
Without memory there is no reason to expect overfitting to produce negative performance (AMS 2014). With memory, the configuration that fit history best is the one most likely to be wrong when it reverses. This bench models only the first case, so the honest sentence is "earns nothing, or worse with memory".

What is sqrt(2 ln N) in the AMS paper, and how does it compare with the bench at N = 100?

A · The exact expected maximum of N null trials, and the bench matches it to three decimals
B · The minimum Sharpe ratio a strategy must report to be funded
C · An upper bound to the expected maximum in units of one estimate's standard deviation; at N = 100 it is 3.035 against a measured 2.539 and equation 1's 2.531
The paper writes it as an upper bound, and on every rung it sits above the measurement. It describes how fast the maximum grows, which is why it is quoted loosely as the maximum itself. Quote it as a bound with the measured column beside it.

Marlow Street's backtest reports 1.8 on two years of training data, after about a hundred variants. Why is that exactly what the best of 100 on nothing prints?

A · Because ladder A measures the best in-sample Sharpe ratio of 100 rules with no alpha at 1.80 over 200 worlds of the same length, and SR*, the expected best of 100 from equation 1, is also 1.80, so the backtest sits exactly where noise plus selection puts it
B · Because 1.8 is above the 95 percent line of 1.17, so it is a genuine finding
C · Because any Sharpe ratio above 1 is unusual on two years of data
1.17 is the 95 percent line for one estimate. The 1.8 is the best of about a hundred, and the best of a hundred on nothing averages 1.80 at this sample length. The backtest is indistinguishable from the selection loop running on an empty world.

Homework

About forty minutes, in the console or in Python.

Change the history and watch the ruler move

Run the N = 100 rung with days: 1512 added to the options and a seed0 of your own, so 1,007 training observations instead of 503. Your numbers will differ from any canon figure. Convert the best in-sample figure to rulers with the square root of 1,007 and check whether the maximum in rulers stayed near equation 1 while the annualised figure fell.

Count your own N

Take one analysis you shipped in the last year, in any field, and reconstruct how many variants were tried before the one you reported: features, filters, windows, thresholds. Write the number down, then write down how many of those were genuinely different. Session 4 needs both numbers.

Cheat sheet

The one sentence. The best of N is a different statistic from one estimate, and it cannot be read without N.
The ladder. Best of 10, 100 and 1,000 on nothing: 1.04, 1.80, 2.33 in-sample; 0.14, -0.06, 0.02 out of sample; 200 worlds each.
