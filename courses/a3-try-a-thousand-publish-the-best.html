Try a thousand things, publish the best

Every research team tries many things and shows the best one. On a product experiment that habit costs a little. On a backtest it can cost everything, because the best of many rules scored on the same history reports a high number whether or not anything is there. Tonight measures exactly how high, sets the bar a result has to clear once you know how many rules were tried, and gives Marlow Street, the constructed desk from session 1, the one question that has to come before the 1.8 is read at all. Synthetic data only, and nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Marlow Street, counting the rules nobody wrote down

Marlow Street is a constructed desk: four researchers, one head, a budget for two strategies a year, and a backtested Sharpe ratio of 1.8 still waiting for a decision. Session 1 said to ask how many rules were tried. Session 2 said the market makes any edge harder to keep. Tonight the head gets the answer to the first question, about a hundred, and needs to know what to do with it. The generator behind every session can say exactly what the best of a hundred rules looks like when none of them knows anything, because there the truth is known.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A measured picture of what selection does to a number: the best of 10, 100 and 1,000 random rules on a random walk, in-sample and afterwards. The reason the bar has to rise with the number of trials, and two serious ways of raising it, one fixed and one that moves with N, taught side by side. And one question to put before any backtest figure, with a path for what to do with the answer. Synthetic data only; nothing here is advice on what to buy, sell or trade.

LiveTwo numbers, and only one was on the slide3 min▶

Every backtest result is really two numbers. The first is the headline: the Sharpe ratio of the rule that was kept. The second is N: how many rules, parameter settings and variants were tried on the same history before that one was kept. The first is always on the slide. The second almost never is, and without it the first cannot be read.

That is not a matter of trust. A researcher who tried a hundred rules and showed the best has done ordinary research. The problem is arithmetic: the maximum of many noisy estimates is larger than any one of them, and it keeps growing as N grows, whether or not any rule has an edge. Tonight puts numbers on that growth and on the bar it implies.

Real world

Marlow Street (constructed) asked the researcher for N. The researcher counted the rules saved in the notebook, then remembered the variants tried and discarded in the first week, and the parameter sweeps that were never saved. The honest answer was "about a hundred, maybe more". That uncertainty is normal. It is also the reason the head now writes N on the proposal before anyone discusses the headline.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • N. The number of trials: every rule, setting and variant scored on the same history, including the ones nobody wrote down.
  • In-sample and out-of-sample. The stretch used to choose the rule, and a later stretch it never saw. On these pages: the first 503 days, then the next 252.
  • Best of N. The rule with the highest in-sample Sharpe ratio among N. Its in-sample figure is the one that gets presented.
  • Expected maximum. The average best-of-N figure when none of the N rules has any edge. It is the hurdle a result must clear before selection is ruled out as the explanation.
  • Naive test. A one-sided 95 percent test of the kept rule against zero, as if it were the only rule tried.
  • Deflated test. The same test against the expected maximum of N instead of zero. The idea comes from Bailey and Lopez de Prado (2014); the formula is practitioner session 4.
  • t-statistic. An estimate divided by its own standard deviation. Sharpe (1994): the t-statistic equals the Sharpe ratio times the square root of the number of observations.
  • Constructed. Marlow Street and every figure here come from a generator whose truth is known. No figure describes a real market.
Part 1 · covers selection on the same data

The best of a crowd that knows nothing

15 min live

Put N rules into a random walk with no alpha at all. Score every one of them on the first 503 days, keep the best, and then watch the kept rule on the 252 days that follow. The figure draws that machinery, and the place it goes wrong: the loop that chooses the rule runs back over exactly the days the rule is then scored on.

LiveWhat the best of N reports, and what it earns5 min▶

Run the experiment on a random walk with no alpha, 200 worlds per rung, and keep the best of N rules on the first 503 days. The best in-sample Sharpe ratio is -0.01 with one rule, 1.04 with ten, 1.80 with a hundred and 2.33 with a thousand. The same kept rule, scored on the 252 days it never saw, earns -0.07, 0.14, -0.06 and 0.02. The first column climbs with N and does not stop. The second never leaves the noise around zero, because there was nothing to find.

Now apply the ordinary test a data team would use: a one-sided 95 percent test of the kept rule against zero. It passes in 6.0 percent of worlds with one rule, which is the 5 percent false-positive rate it promises, within trial noise. With ten rules it passes in 32.5 percent. With a hundred, 99.5 percent. With a thousand, all 200. Every one of those passes is false, because every rule in every world knows nothing.

Real world

Marlow Street (constructed) was handed a 1.8 from about a hundred rules on two years of history. The best of 100 rules on nothing, on 503 days, prints 1.80 averaged over 200 worlds. The headline is not too good to be true; it is exactly what the search produces when the truth is zero, and the ordinary test would have called it a finding in 199 of those 200 worlds.

LiveTwenty trials, seven trials, forty-five configurations4 min▶

Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado and Zhu put the arithmetic in front of mathematicians in 2014, in the Notices of the American Mathematical Society, read at source. Three of their statements are worth carrying into any research review.

  • Twenty trials. At a 5 percent false-positive rate, "after only twenty trials, the researcher is expected to find one specification that passes". A test calibrated for one look is spent after twenty.
  • Seven trials. "After trying only seven independent strategy configurations, the expected maximum SR IS is 1 for a two-year long backtest, while the expected SR OOS is 0." The generator's ten-rule rung agrees: 1.04 in-sample, 0.14 afterwards.
  • Forty-five configurations. With five years of data, "no more than forty-five independent model configurations should be tried". Their minimum backtest length runs the same logic in reverse: the more you try, the more independent history you need before the best result means anything.

None of this needs a market to be true. It is a property of taking the maximum of noisy numbers, which is why a random walk demonstrates it as well as any history could.

Self-studyWhy the kept rule earns zero here, not less3 min▶

A common way to say all this is "overfit strategies lose money". The generator does not show that, and the paper does not claim it unconditionally. The AMS authors write that "in the absence of memory, there is no reason to expect overfitting to induce negative performance". With memory in the strategy's performance series, they describe a compensation effect, in which a rule is more likely to be selected in-sample precisely because it is about to underperform out of sample.

The generator behind these pages draws independent returns, so it is the no-memory case, and every zero-alpha rung reports an out-of-sample mean within noise of zero: 0.14, -0.06 and 0.02 at N of 10, 100 and 1,000. The honest sentence is "overfit rules earn nothing, or worse if the series has memory, which this generator does not model". Earning nothing is still a real loss: the capital was tied up, and the budget for a better rule was spent.

Part 2 · covers the expected maximum and the hurdle

Raise the bar to the best of nothing

10 min live

If the best of N rules on nothing reaches a predictable height, then that height is the bar a real result has to clear. It rises with N, and the figure draws how fast: the measured best of N in units of one estimate's spread, the textbook bound above it, and the fixed bar of t above 3 that Harvey, Liu and Zhu proposed for a crowded literature.

N rules on a random walk with no alpha, the best one kept on the first 503 days and scored on the next 252, averaged over 200 worlds at the canon seeds. Then the kept rule is tested twice: against zero, and against the best of N on nothing.

LiveThe expected maximum, and the bound above it3 min▶

Measure the best of N in units of one estimate's own standard deviation and the generator, over 200 worlds per rung, reports 1.475 at N = 10, 2.539 at 100 and 3.291 at 1,000. Multiply by the ruler from session 2, 0.71 by formula on 503 days, and those become the annualised 1.04, 1.80 and 2.33 of Part 1. Marlow Street's 1.8 is about 2.5 rulers from zero, which is where the best of a hundred lands on nothing.

The figure everyone half-remembers is sqrt(2 ln N): 2.146, 3.035 and 3.717 for the same three values of N. The AMS paper gives it as "an upper bound", not as the estimate, and the generator shows why the distinction matters: at N = 100 the bound says 3.04 and the measured maximum is 2.54. The expected maximum grows like sqrt(2 ln N); it does not equal it. Quote both, and say which is which.

The sharper formula from the deflated Sharpe paper lands within 0.1 of the measured column at N = 10 and within 0.04 at 100 and 1,000. Practitioner session 3 builds both and checks them against the generator.

LiveThe t above 3 argument3 min▶

Harvey, Liu and Zhu counted the literature instead of a single desk: 313 papers proposing 316 factors that claim to explain expected returns, read at source in The Review of Financial Studies 29(1), 2016. Their conclusion is a bar, stated plainly: "A new factor needs to clear a much higher hurdle, with a t-statistic greater than 3.0". A t of 3.0 corresponds to a p-value of 0.27 percent, against the 5 percent most teams were trained on.

They go further than the bar. They write that "most claimed research findings in financial economics are likely false", and they argue there are good reasons to expect that 3.0 is itself too low. On the figure it is the burgundy line: fixed, simple to apply, and the same for everyone.

Real world

Marlow Street (constructed) tried the fixed bar first. On 503 days the 1.8 is about 2.5 in t units, short of 3.0, so the proposal fails it. The head liked how quick that was. Then a second researcher pointed out that a proposal from a ten-rule search and one from a thousand-rule search face the same bar, and the generator's best of 1,000 on nothing averages 3.29. The head kept the bar and added a second question.

LiveThe deflated Sharpe idea, without the formula3 min▶

Bailey and Lopez de Prado (2014) turn the expected maximum into the test itself. Instead of asking whether the kept rule beats zero, ask whether it beats what the best of N would report if every rule knew nothing. Their deflated Sharpe ratio is, in their words, a probabilistic Sharpe ratio "where the rejection threshold is adjusted to reflect the multiplicity of trials", and it uses five additional inputs: skewness, kurtosis, the sample length, the variance of the Sharpe ratios tested, and the number of independent trials. The leader's version is one sentence: the hurdle is the expected best of N on nothing.

On the zero-alpha ladder the deflated test passes in none of the 200 worlds at N = 10, 100 or 1,000, where the naive test passed in 32.5, 99.5 and 100 percent. That is the promise kept. The price is power. Planted among 100 rules on three years, a real Sharpe ratio of 1.5 almost never survives: 0 of 200 worlds in ladder B, 1.5 percent of 200 in ladder B2 at N = 100, and 3 percent of 100 worlds in ladder B3. Deflation does not invent false findings, and on short history it will not confirm most real ones either.

The widget above prints both tests side by side. At N = 1 they give the same answer, because with one rule there is nothing to deflate for.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

One position: a fixed, high bar. Harvey, Liu and Zhu set t above 3.0 for a new factor and argue it may be too low. A fixed bar is easy to audit, cannot be tuned after the fact, and does not depend on anyone reporting N honestly, which in practice nobody can do perfectly.

The other position: a bar that moves with N. Bailey and Lopez de Prado deflate against the expected maximum of the trials actually run, adjusted for skew, kurtosis and sample length. A bar tied to N is fairer to a narrow search and harsher on a wide one, but it is only as good as the count of trials, including the ones nobody wrote down.

The deflated Sharpe paper itself calls the Harvey and Liu style threshold and its own deflated test complementary, and encourages readers to compute both. That is how to hold it: two serious answers to one question, each with a cost. A reviewer can report both numbers for every proposal and let the disagreement between them be part of the evidence. Neither camp has shown the other wrong, and this page does not pick.

Part 3 · build-along

The question that comes before the number

17 min hands on

No code. Six steps Marlow Street's head now runs on the 1.8, and that you can run on any result your own team presents. The figure draws them as a path: three questions to answer before anyone reads the headline, one comparison, and the evidence that would change the answer.

Ask how many were tried, including the ones nobody wrote down

Before the headline is discussed, get N on the page. Count saved rules, then parameter sweeps, then variants abandoned in the first week, then anything a colleague tried on the same history. Marlow Street's researcher started with the saved notebook and ended at "about a hundred, maybe more". When the count is uncertain, write the larger number; the bar only gets more lenient if you guess low.

Ask how long the sample is

How many observations the rule was chosen on, and how many of them are independent. Marlow Street's head writes 503 days. Overlapping holding periods count for less than their row count suggests, and session 4 shows how a shuffled split hides that. The answer fixes the size of the ruler: 0.71 per estimate on 503 days, by formula.

Read the hurdle for that N

The hurdle is the expected best of N on nothing over the same sample. On 503 days the generator gives 1.04 at N = 10, 1.80 at 100 and 2.33 at 1,000, each a mean of 200 worlds, and the deflated Sharpe formula's version of the hurdle is 1.07, 1.80 and 2.31. Write the one that matches your N beside the headline, and the fixed bar of t above 3.0 beside that.

Compare, and say the gap out loud

Marlow Street: a headline of 1.8 against a hurdle of 1.80 at N = 100. The gap is zero. Selection on its own explains the whole number, and on the zero-alpha ladder the deflated test passes in none of 200 worlds at that N. In t units the 1.8 is about 2.5 against a fixed bar of 3.0, so it fails that test too. Two serious bars, one verdict here; on another proposal they may disagree, and then you report both.

Decide what evidence would change the answer

The cure is more independent history, which cannot be manufactured. Ladder B3 plants a real Sharpe ratio of 1.5 among 100 rules and varies the history, 100 worlds each: it passes deflation in 3 percent of worlds on 3 years, 6 percent on 6, 45 percent on 12 and 94 percent on 24. Ask what history the claim could be tested on that nobody has read yet, and how long it would take to arrive.

Write one line for the memo

N, the sample length, the hurdle, the gap, and the evidence that would move the verdict. Marlow Street's reads: "About 100 rules on 503 days; hurdle 1.80; headline 1.8; gap zero; revisit only with history the search never touched." Then compare it with the guess you committed to in session 1. On the generator the best of 100 earns -0.06 in the year after, a mean of 200 worlds.

The habit to keep. Never read a backtest number before you have written down N. The number is only meaningful as a distance from the best of N on nothing, and that distance is the finding. A 1.8 that clears the best of ten by a wide margin is worth a second look. A 1.8 that equals the best of a hundred is the search reporting on itself.

Check yourself

Three questions

Marlow Street's researcher confirms that about 100 rules were tried on 503 days before the 1.8 was kept. What is the honest reading?

A · Strong evidence, because the ordinary one-sided 95 percent test passes
B · No evidence beyond selection: the best of 100 rules on nothing reports 1.80 on 503 days, averaged over 200 worlds, and earns -0.06 afterwards
C · Half of it is real, so expect about 0.9 in the year after funding
The naive test passes in 99.5 percent of zero-alpha worlds at N = 100, so its pass says nothing. 1.80 is the hurdle itself, and the gap is zero. Option C invents a split the data does not support: on the generator the kept rule earns nothing, not half.

At N = 100, sqrt(2 ln N) gives 3.04 and the generator measures the best of N on nothing at 2.54, in units of one estimate's standard deviation. How should the two be quoted?

A · Quote 3.04, because the formula is exact and the generator is noisy
B · Quote 2.54 only, because the formula is wrong
C · sqrt(2 ln N) is an upper bound and the expected maximum grows like it; quote both and say which is the bound and which is measured
The AMS paper calls sqrt(2 ln N) an upper bound, not the estimate. The measured column is a mean over 200 worlds, and the sharper deflated Sharpe formula lands within 0.04 of it at N = 100. Neither number is wrong; they answer different questions.

One reviewer wants every new result to clear t above 3.0. Another wants it deflated against the expected best of N. Which is right?

A · Both are serious positions; the deflated Sharpe paper calls them complementary, and a reviewer can report both
B · t above 3.0, because it is simpler and so it must be better
C · Deflation, because it is newer and makes a fixed bar obsolete
A fixed bar is easy to audit and does not depend on an honest count of trials; a bar that moves with N is fairer to narrow searches and harsher on wide ones. Harvey, Liu and Zhu even argue 3.0 may be too low. The literature has not settled it, and neither should a review.
Before session 4

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one result your own team has reported and on the Marlow Street guess you have been carrying since session 1.

Count N for one real result

Pick one headline your team presented: a model, a forecast, a feature, anything chosen from alternatives. List every alternative tried on the same data, including the discarded ones. Write the count, then write it again as the larger number you would accept if pressed.

Write the memo line for it

N, the sample length, the hurdle, the gap, and the evidence that would move the verdict. If you cannot fill in the hurdle for your metric, write "unknown" and note that the absence is itself the finding: nobody has measured what the search alone produces.

Settle your Marlow Street guess

Put your session 1 number, and the revised one from session 2, beside the generator's answer: the best of 100 on nothing earns -0.06 in the 252 days after, a mean of 200 worlds. Write one sentence on why your guess was higher, if it was. Session 4 adds the second way a backtest misleads: information leaking from where it should not.

Cheat sheet

The one sentence. The best of N rules reports a high number whether or not anything is there.
