A strategy as a function

A backtest number is the output of four small pieces of arithmetic: a signal, a position, a profit and loss line, and a ratio. Most arguments about a backtest are really arguments about one of the four that nobody wrote down. Tonight writes all four down, reads the engine's own lines for each, and finds the one assumption that hides inside the square root of time. The data is synthetic throughout, and nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Marlow Street asks for the function, not the number

Marlow Street is a constructed research desk, four people and a head who has been handed a backtest with an annualised Sharpe ratio of 1.8 and asked whether to fund it. Session 1 built the world where nothing is there. Tonight the head asks a narrower question before any statistics: write the strategy down as a function. What does it read, what position does it take, how long does it hold, what does a change of mind cost, and how does the ratio get from one number per day to one number per year? Every number on this page comes from a seeded generator of synthetic data, and nothing here is investment advice.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A strategy written as a function from what was knowable at t to a position of +1 or -1, held for h days, with the engine's own pnl() read line by line. A cost line that is charged on the change in position, never on the position. A Sharpe ratio built from scratch with moments() and annualise(), and the reason the scale is the square root of 252 over h. And one honest caveat: the square root of time assumes independent observations, and five-day windows that overlap are not independent.

LiveWhy write the strategy as a function2 min▶

A function has a domain and a range, and both are checkable. The domain is everything the strategy was allowed to know when it decided: the signal up to the close of day t and nothing after. The range is a position: +1, long one unit, or -1, short one unit. Everything a reviewer needs to ask about leakage, costs and scaling is a question about one of those two sides.

Written this way the strategy stops being a chart and becomes four lines anyone can re-run. That is the point. A number that cannot be recomputed from a written function cannot be reviewed, only believed.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Signal. A number per day the strategy reads. Here, an AR(1) series with phi 0.8, one per strategy, that knows nothing about the returns unless it is the planted one.
  • Position. The sign of the signal: +1 when it is zero or above, -1 below. One unit, no sizing until session 9.
  • Hold, h. How many days each observation's return covers. h = 1 is a daily strategy; h = 5 earns a five-day forward return per observation.
  • Observation. One value of the PnL line, y[t]. With h = 1 there are 755 of them on a 756-day world; with h = 5, 751.
  • Cost line. Basis points charged per unit of position change. A flip from +1 to -1 is two units.
  • Per-observation Sharpe. Mean of y over its sample standard deviation, with no time scale attached.
  • Annualised Sharpe. The per-observation figure times the square root of 252 over h.
  • Knowledge cut-off. The close of day t. A signal that reads anything after it is the lookahead fault session 8 plants.

Part 1 · covers signal, position and PnL

From what was knowable at t to one observation of profit and loss

10 min live

The engine's strategy is four steps and each one is a single line. Read a signal at the close of day t. Take its sign. Hold that position for the next h days and collect their summed return. Subtract a cost for every unit the position moved since the last observation. The result is one number per day, y[t], and every statistic in the next eight sessions is computed from that line and nothing else.

LiveThe signal: an AR(1) that knows nothing3 min▶

makeStrategies(world, { N, seed }) makes N signals, one per strategy, from its own seed: the world seed plus 1000 by convention. Each starts at a standard normal and then follows x[t] = 0.8 times x[t-1] plus 0.6 times a fresh normal. The 0.6 is the square root of one minus 0.8 squared, which keeps the signal's variance at one every day.

None of these signals is computed from the returns. They are drawn from a different random stream, so on a zero-alpha world every one of them is a coin flip dressed up as a rule. The only exception is the planted one: when plantIndex is set, that strategy's signal is the world's hidden signal itself, the one the returns lean on. Session 6 switches that on; tonight it stays off.

Why AR(1) and not plain noise? Because 0.8 makes the sign persist. The probability that an AR(1) with phi 0.8 changes sign on a given day is arccos(0.8) over pi, about 0.205, so a position lasts a few days on average. That persistence is what makes the cost line and overlapping windows mean something.

LiveThe position: a sign, held for h days2 min▶

Inside pnl() the position at observation t is signal[t] >= 0 ? 1 : -1. A signal of exactly zero goes long; that tie never matters on continuous data but it is written down, which is the point. There is no sizing: every position is one unit. Session 9 multiplies it by a leverage and watches the mean and the median part ways.

The position reads signal[t - shift], and the shift defaults to zero. That one parameter is the knowledge cut-off in code. With shift 0 the signal at the close of day t trades days t+1 onwards. With shift -1 it would read day t+1's signal before trading day t+1, which is tomorrow leaking into today. Session 8 plants exactly that fault in a five-day momentum rule and, over 200 worlds of nothing, the Sharpe ratio goes from 0.02 to 6.11.

Real world

Marlow Street, constructed like the rest, asked the author of the 1.8 backtest to fill in a one-page template: inputs, the cut-off, the position rule, h, the cost. The first draft could not be filled in. Its position for a day read a column that the data team updated the morning after. Nobody had lied. The function had simply never been written, so nobody had looked at which side of the steel line each input sat on.

LiveThe PnL: one observation per day, overlapping when h > 13 min▶

pnl(world, signal, { h, costBp, shift }) makes T = days minus h observations. For each t from 0 to T - 1 it takes the position, sums the next h returns r[t+1] to r[t+h] into a forward return, multiplies, and subtracts the cost line. That is the whole function. On a 756-day world, h = 1 gives 755 observations and h = 5 gives 751.

With h = 1 each observation earns one new day and no two observations share a day. With h = 5 observation t earns days t+1 to t+5 and observation t+1 earns days t+2 to t+6: four of the five days are the same. The engine still makes one observation per day, so 751 observations are built from the same 755 days a daily strategy uses. They are not 751 independent five-day bets.

Overlap is not a bug. It is how a strategy that holds for a week is honestly scored every day. It becomes a bug in two places: when the observations are counted as if they were independent (Part 2), and when a shuffled split puts observation t in training and t+1 in testing, so the test shares four days with the training set (session 5).

Self-studyThe cost line: charged on the change, not the position3 min▶

The engine's line is y[t] = pos * fwd - cost * Math.abs(pos - prev), where cost is costBp over 10,000 and prev is the previous observation's position. Holding the same position costs nothing. Flipping from +1 to -1 is a change of two units and pays twice the cost. The first observation starts from prev = 0, so it pays one unit to open.

The arithmetic follows from the signal. A sign change on about 20.5 percent of days, at two units a flip, is about 0.41 units of position change a day. Each 5 basis points then costs about 0.32 of annualised Sharpe at one percent daily volatility. Session 7 measures the consequence on a real edge: a planted Sharpe of 1.0 falls to 0.69 in-sample at 5 basis points, a mean over 200 worlds, and is negative at 20.

The cost is charged once per observation, at the moment the position changes, also when h = 5. That is a choice, written down, and a reviewer can disagree with it. What a reviewer cannot do is check a cost that was never placed on a specific line.

Part 2 · covers the Sharpe ratio from scratch

Mean over standard deviation, then one square root

10 min live

The Sharpe ratio is two lines in the engine. moments() takes the PnL line and returns its mean, its sample standard deviation and their ratio, which is the Sharpe ratio per observation. annualise() multiplies that by the square root of 252 over h. The first line is plain arithmetic. The second carries an assumption, and the assumption is exactly what overlapping windows break.

Livemoments(): the ratio per observation3 min▶

moments(y) computes the mean of y, then the sample variance with n - 1 in the denominator, its square root as sd, and sr = mean over sd. It also returns skewness and kurtosis (not excess) from the population moments, which session 4 feeds into the probabilistic Sharpe ratio. Nothing else. There is no risk-free rate to subtract: the position is long or short one unit with no financing, so the PnL line is already the excess return the ratio wants.

Read the box above with World 1, h = 1 and no cost. The per-observation mean is a few hundredths of a percent, the standard deviation sits near the one percent daily volatility, and their ratio is a small number with no time unit. That small number is the Sharpe ratio. Everything after it is a change of units.

Liveannualise(): why the square root of 252 over h3 min▶

If observations were independent with mean m and standard deviation s, a year of them would have mean m times the count and standard deviation s times the square root of the count. Their ratio grows by the square root of the count. With h = 1 a year holds 252 observations, so the scale is the square root of 252, about 15.87. With h = 5 each observation spans five days, so a year holds 252 over 5 non-overlapping ones and the scale is the square root of 50.4, about 7.10. That is the engine's annualise(sr, h) = sr * Math.sqrt(252 / h).

The scale puts h = 1 and h = 5 on the same yearly footing for the size of the ratio. It does nothing about the evidence. The same square root reappears in Sharpe 1994 as the link to significance: the t-statistic equals the Sharpe ratio times the square root of T. With 751 overlapping observations, T is not 751. On 755 days there are 151 non-overlapping five-day windows, and any test that counts 751 treats one week's evidence as if it were seen five times.

Real world

Marlow Street, constructed, rebuilt the 1.8 from its spreadsheet. The strategy held for a week and was scored daily, which is fine, but the ratio had been annualised by the square root of 252, as if each five-day observation were one day. That inflates the figure by the square root of 5, about 2.24, before anyone has asked a statistical question. Writing the function with h as a named input made the mistake impossible to miss, and the head stopped reading the number until it was recomputed.

Self-studySharpe 1966 and 1994: the name, ex ante and ex post3 min▶

Sharpe's 1994 paper recalls that he introduced the measure in 1966 as the "reward-to-variability ratio", and adopts the name Sharpe Ratio from the usage that had grown up around it. It separates two versions. Ex ante: the expected differential return over its predicted standard deviation, a forecast. Ex post: the historic average differential return over its historic standard deviation, a measurement. Every number a backtest prints is ex post. Funding a strategy is a bet on the ex ante figure. The gap between the two is what the next eight sessions are about.

Two sentences from the paper carry tonight. First, "The Sharpe Ratio is not independent of the time period over which it is measured", which is why a ratio quoted without its h and its annualisation cannot be compared with another. Second, "The t-statistic will equal the Sharpe Ratio times the square root of T", which ties the ratio to significance and to the sample length, and is why session 1's ruler was 0.71 by the formula, the square root of 252 over 503.

Sharpe, W. F. (1994). "The Sharpe Ratio." The Journal of Portfolio Management, Fall 1994. Read at source: https://web.stanford.edu/~wfsharpe/art/sr/sr.htm

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

The case for the square root of time. Annualising by the square root of the number of periods in a year is the convention almost every report uses. It puts strategies scored at different frequencies on one scale, it is exact when observations are independent with a stable mean and variance, and a reader who knows the convention can undo it. A convention everyone applies the same way is useful even where it is imperfect.

The case against. The rule is only exact under independence, and many series are not independent: overlapping windows like h = 5 here, positions that persist, returns with memory. Sharpe 1994 itself warns that the ratio "is not independent of the time period over which it is measured". On this view a ratio should be reported at the frequency it was measured, with the sample length and the overlap stated, and any yearly figure treated as a unit conversion rather than as evidence.

Both positions are held by careful people and the literature has not settled which reporting rule is better. The engine takes the first position for the scale and teaches the second for the evidence: annualise() multiplies by the square root of 252 over h, and session 5 removes the overlap from training sets by purging rather than pretending it is not there. That is a stated choice, not a resolution.

Part 3 · build-along

Write the function, score it, then score it the canon way

22 min hands on

Open this page's console. The engine is loaded as QT. Six steps take one random rule on one zero-alpha world from a seed to an annualised Sharpe ratio, twice, then repeat the first version over 200 worlds. This is the exercise Marlow Street, constructed, now asks of every backtest before it is discussed: same function, re-run by someone else, same number.

Make one world and one rule

Seed 10001 is World 1 in the box above, and the first world of the canon rung in step 6. The rule's seed is the world seed plus 1000, the convention every page uses. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

consolevar w = QT.makeWorld({ trueSR: 0, seed: 10001 }); var x = QT.makeStrategies(w, { N: 1, seed: w.seed + 1000 })[0]; x.slice(0, 5)

Turn the rule into a PnL line

h = 1 and no cost. Check the length: 755 observations from 756 days, because the last day has no tomorrow to earn.

consolevar y = QT.pnl(w, x, { h: 1 }); y.length

Take the moments of the first 503 observations

Mean, sample standard deviation and their ratio, the per-observation Sharpe ratio. Your console prints the same figures as the box's top rows for World 1, h = 1, no cost; another seed prints other figures.

consolevar m = QT.moments(y.slice(0, 503)); [m.n, m.mean.toFixed(5), m.sd.toFixed(5), m.sr.toFixed(4)]

Annualise it, in and out of sample

Multiply by the square root of 252 over h, with h = 1. Then do the same on observations 503 onwards, the out-of-sample stretch. Your numbers will match for the same seed; the figures themselves are one draw and mean nothing alone.

consolevar mOut = QT.moments(y.slice(503)); [QT.annualise(m.sr, 1).toFixed(2), QT.annualise(mOut.sr, 1).toFixed(2)]

Same rule, h = 5 and 5 basis points

Four fewer observations, a five-day forward return each, a cost on every change. Annualise with h = 5 this time. Then count the units of position change per observation and compare with the arithmetic in Part 1, about 0.41 a day.

consolevar y5 = QT.pnl(w, x, { h: 5, costBp: 5 }); var m5 = QT.moments(y5.slice(0, 503)); var turn = 0, prev = 0; for (var t = 0; t < y5.length; t++) { var p = x[t] >= 0 ? 1 : -1; turn += Math.abs(p - prev); prev = p; } [y5.length, QT.annualise(m5.sr, 5).toFixed(2), (turn / y5.length).toFixed(2)]

Now the canon: 200 worlds

ladderRung repeats steps 1 to 4 over seeds 10001 to 10200 (seed0 10000, the canon seed for this rung) and reports means. It prints -0.01 in-sample and -0.07 out of sample. Your numbers will match, exactly, because the seeds are the canon seeds.

consolevar r = QT.ladderRung({ N: 1, trueSR: 0, method: "walkforward" }, 200, 10000); [r.trainSize, r.isSR.toFixed(2), r.oosSR.toFixed(2)]

The habit to keep. Before you argue about a Sharpe ratio, write the function that produced it: the cut-off, the position rule, h, the cost line and the annualisation. Then have someone else re-run it from the seed. If either step fails, the number is not ready to be discussed.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why does the engine annualise a per-observation Sharpe ratio by the square root of 252 over h?

The engine makes one observation per day whatever h is, so A is wrong about the count. The scale assumes independent periods: the mean grows with the count and the standard deviation with its square root. That puts the size of the ratio on a yearly footing; it does not make overlapping observations independent.

In pnl(), what is the cost line charged on?

A trade happens when the position changes, so that is where the cost goes. With phi 0.8 the sign changes on about 20.5 percent of days, about 0.41 units of change a day, which is why a few basis points move the ratio as much as session 7 shows.

Why do the observations overlap when h = 5?

The world has the same 756 daily returns whatever h is. Scoring a five-day hold every day reuses each day in up to five observations, so the observations are correlated and 751 of them are nowhere near 751 independent five-day bets; 755 days hold 151 non-overlapping windows.

Take this with you

Cheat sheet

The one sentence. A strategy is a function from what was knowable at t to a position; if it cannot be written that way, it cannot be checked.
The cut-off. The close of day t. The position reads to its left and earns to its right. A shift of -1 crosses it.
The position. sign(x[t]): +1 at zero or above, -1 below. One unit, held h days.
The PnL line. y[t] = position[t] times r[t+1] + ... + r[t+h], less the cost on the change. T = days minus h.
The cost. Charged on |position[t] - position[t-1]|. Holding is free; a flip is two units; about 0.41 units a day here.
The ratio. Mean over sample sd, per observation. No risk-free rate: the long or short unit is already an excess return.
The scale. Times sqrt(252 / h): 15.87 at h = 1, 7.10 at h = 5. Right for units, silent on evidence.
The caveat. t = SR times sqrt(T) (Sharpe 1994) needs independent observations. At h = 5, 755 days hold 151 non-overlapping windows, not 751.
