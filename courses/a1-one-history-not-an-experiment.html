Learn Quant Trading with Phoebe · Leader session 1 of 6

A backtest is one history, not an experiment

A data team knows how to read an experiment: a control group, a random split, a result you can rerun on a fresh sample. A backtest has none of those. It is one realised path of history, read once, by somebody who tried many rules on that same path and brought you the best one. Marlow Street, a constructed four-person research desk, has been handed a strategy with an annualised Sharpe ratio of 1.8 and asked whether to fund it. Tonight is what that number is, what it is not, and the three questions that separate a finding from a selection. Nothing here is investment advice; the data is generated so that the truth is known.

Part 0

Marlow Street, on the morning a backtest lands on the desk

Marlow Street is a constructed desk: four researchers, one head, and a budget that funds two strategies a year. A researcher presents a rule with a backtested Sharpe ratio of 1.8 over two years. The head has run product experiments for a decade and knows exactly what a result like that would mean on an A/B test. The trouble is that none of that knowledge transfers, because the thing on the screen is not an experiment, and the ways in which it is not one are the whole subject of the six leader sessions.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A clear statement of what a backtest is: one path, no control, no rerun, and selected. What a Sharpe ratio is and how noisy one estimate of it is on two years of daily data, which is more than most people expect. A measured answer to the question "what does the best of a hundred random rules look like", which is the question Marlow Street's head should have asked first. LiveWhy the experiment instinct misfires here4 min▶

An experiment has three properties a data leader relies on without naming them. There is a control: a group that did not get the treatment, drawn from the same population at the same time. There is a fresh draw: if the result looks odd you can run it again on new users next week. And the design is fixed before the data arrives, so the result is a test of a hypothesis rather than a search for one.

A backtest has one history. There is no control group that lived through a different past. There is no second draw, because the past happened once. And the rule was almost always chosen after looking at that history, which means the design was fitted to the data it is now being scored on. Each of those on its own would be a caveat. All three together make the number on the screen a different kind of object from an experimental result, and the rest of the leader track is what kind.

Real world

Marlow Street's head asked the researcher the question she would ask of any experiment: what is the p-value? The researcher gave one, and it was small. Neither of them asked how many rules had been tried before this one, and the honest answer was somewhere above a hundred. That omission is not dishonesty. It is the ordinary way research is reported, and the papers in the sources section are the people who first counted its cost.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Backtest. A simulation of what a rule would have earned had it been run over a stretch of history. In-sample is the stretch used to design the rule; out-of-sample is a stretch it never saw.
  • Sharpe ratio. Mean return in excess of a benchmark, divided by the standard deviation of that excess. Introduced in 1966 as the reward-to-variability ratio and renamed by common usage; the 1994 paper is the one to cite. Annualised by multiplying a daily figure by the square root of 252.
  • Trial. One rule, one parameter setting, one backtest. The count of trials is the number nobody writes down.
  • Selection. Picking the best of N trials on the same data. Session 3 measures what it does to the number.
  • Leakage. Information from the future, or from outside the sample, reaching the rule. Session 4 names its classes.
  • Zero alpha. A series with no exploitable structure at all. Every number on these pages is first measured on such a series, so that whatever a method reports there is known to be noise.
  • Constructed. Every case here, Marlow Street included, is invented so that the truth is knowable. No figure describes a real market.
Part 1 · covers what a backtest is

One path, no control, no rerun, and chosen

15 min live

The clearest way to see what a backtest lacks is to draw the experiment beside it. On the left, the machinery a data team trusts: a population, a random split, a comparison, and an arrow that says do it again. On the right, the machinery a backtest actually has. Three parts are missing and one has been added, and the one that was added points backwards.

LiveWhat a Sharpe ratio is, and how noisy one estimate of it is5 min▶

The ratio is the mean of the strategy's returns in excess of a benchmark, divided by their standard deviation. Sharpe's 1994 paper gives the ex ante form (expected differential return over its predicted standard deviation) and the ex post form (the historic average over the historic standard deviation), and says plainly that the ratio "is not independent of the time period over which it is measured". A daily figure is annualised by the square root of 252, which is why a number like 1.8 is quoted as if it were a yearly fact.

The part leaders rarely hear is the standard error. On 503 daily observations, roughly two years, one estimate of an annualised Sharpe ratio on a series with no alpha at all has a standard deviation of about 0.71. That is the width of the noise before anybody has selected anything. A single rule on a single path reporting 1.0 is one and a half standard deviations from nothing. Sharpe gives the same fact from the other side: "The t-statistic will equal the Sharpe Ratio times the square root of T", and T here is small.

The practitioner track builds that estimate from scratch in session 2 and measures its noise in session 1. For a leader the number to keep is 0.71: it is the ruler every backtest figure is held against.

LiveThree missing parts, named4 min▶

No control. The strategy's return is compared to a benchmark, which is a number, not a group. A benchmark cannot tell you what the strategy would have earned in a world where the rule did not exist, because the world only happened one way. The experiment courses handle this properly; the difference in finance is that there is no way to randomise history.

No rerun. Out-of-sample testing is the closest thing to a fresh draw, and it is one draw, not a fresh draw. Once the out-of-sample stretch has been looked at, it has been used, and the next rule tried on it is in-sample again. The data is consumed by being read.

No fixed design. The rule was chosen by trying rules. That is not a scandal, it is research. The scandal is reporting the best rule's score as if it were the only rule's score, and the paper that counted the cost is the subject of Part 2.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

One camp holds that overfitting a backtest costs you nothing more than the illusion: the rule earns zero afterwards, and the loss is the capital you tied up and the better rule you did not fund. Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado and Zhu say exactly this for series without memory: "in the absence of memory, there is no reason to expect overfitting to induce negative performance".

The same authors hold the other position for series with memory: "the presence of memory in a strategy's performance series induces a compensation effect, which increases the chances for that strategy to be selected IS, only to underperform the rest OOS". On that reading, an overfit rule does not merely earn nothing, it is selected precisely because it is about to give back what it took.

Which case you are in depends on the series, and the honest statement is that the generator behind these pages has no memory, so every zero-alpha rung reports an out-of-sample figure within noise of zero, never below it. A page that says "overfit strategies lose money" has resolved a question the literature left open. Say "earn nothing, or worse if the series has memory".

Part 2 · covers Marlow Street

What the best of a hundred random rules looks like

10 min live

Marlow Street's head should have asked one question first, and it is answerable without any market data at all: if a hundred rules with no information whatsoever were scored on two years of a random walk, what would the best of them report? The generator behind every page here answers it directly, and the answer is a Sharpe ratio of 1.80, which is the number that was presented.

LiveReading the two columns together3 min▶

Set the rules to 100 and the worlds to 200 and the box reports the canon: a best in-sample Sharpe ratio of 1.80 and an out-of-sample figure of -0.06. Set it to 1,000 and the first number climbs to 2.33 while the second stays at 0.02. The in-sample figure is a function of how hard you looked. The out-of-sample figure is a function of what was there, and nothing was.

The researcher who presented 1.8 did not lie and did not cheat. They tried about a hundred things, as researchers do, and showed the best. The leader's job is to know that 1.8 is what the best of a hundred looks like when the hundred know nothing, and to ask for N before asking for anything else.

LiveThe paper that counted it3 min▶

Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado and Zhu made the arithmetic public in 2014 in the Notices of the American Mathematical Society. Two of their sentences are worth carrying around. "After trying only seven independent strategy configurations, the expected maximum SR IS is 1 for a two-year long backtest, while the expected SR OOS is 0." And: "if only five years of data are available, no more than forty-five independent model configurations should be tried", or the search is "almost guaranteed to produce strategies with an annualized Sharpe ratio IS of 1 but an expected Sharpe ratio OOS of zero".

Their bound is that the expected maximum grows like the square root of twice the natural log of N. Session 3 measures that growth on this generator and finds the bound is loose and their sharper formula is tight. For tonight the point is simpler: the growth never stops, so a large enough search produces any Sharpe ratio you ask for, on nothing.

Self-studyWhy "not investment advice" is a technical statement here3 min▶

Every number on these pages comes from a generator whose truth is known: the alpha is zero unless a signal is planted, and when one is planted its strength is a parameter. That is what makes a method's output checkable. It is also what makes the numbers useless as advice. A Sharpe ratio of 1.80 here is a property of a selection procedure run on noise, not a property of anything anybody could buy.

The sibling course on AI in finance holds the rule "assist, never advise", and it applies without change. What a leader takes from here is a way of reading a research claim, not a view on any market. The pages say so wherever money is near, and they mean it as a statement about what the generator can and cannot support.

Part 3 · build-along

Three questions to put to any backtest

17 min hands on

No code. Three questions, asked in order, on a research result your own team has presented or will present. Each one has an answer that can be written down, and the absence of an answer is itself the finding. The figure is the card to keep.

Pick a result your team has presented

A model, a forecast, a strategy, anything with a headline number that went to someone senior. Write the headline number down, and beside it write the number of alternatives that were tried before it. If nobody can produce the second number, write "unknown", and notice how that changes the first.

Ask how many were tried, out loud

Not as an accusation. As the first question. The honest answer is usually larger than the presenter thinks, because parameter sweeps, feature variants and discarded versions are all trials. Count them with the presenter, in the room.

Ask what the split respected

Was the test period after the training period, with a gap? Or were the rows shuffled, which is what every tabular tool does by default? Session 4 draws the mechanism by which a shuffled split leaks; for tonight, write down which one was used.

Ask what a trade cost

If the number is gross of costs, it is not yet a number. Ask for the cost per trade and the number of trades, and multiply. Session 5 shows what that multiplication does to a small edge.

Hold the headline against 0.71

One Sharpe estimate on two years of daily data has a standard deviation of about 0.71 when there is nothing there. Divide the headline by that and say the result out loud. Then remember that the division only applies if N was 1.

Commit to a guess about Marlow Street

The researcher tried about a hundred rules and presented a 1.8. Write down, as a single number, what you expect the strategy to earn in the year after funding, in Sharpe units. Do not hedge it. Session 3 checks the guess against the generator, where the truth is known.

The habit to keep. When someone hands you a backtest, do not ask whether the number is good. Ask what it is the best of. A 1.8 that is the best of one is a finding. A 1.8 that is the best of a hundred is the expected result of looking, and the generator will produce it for you on a random walk any time you like.

Check yourself

Three questions

Marlow Street's researcher presents a rule with a two-year backtested Sharpe ratio of 1.8 and a small p-value. What is the first question the head should ask?

A · Whether the p-value was computed with the right test
B · How many rules were tried before this one, since the best of 100 random rules on a random walk reports 1.80 in-sample and earns nothing afterwards
C · Whether the strategy can be scaled to a larger budget
The p-value is correct for one trial and meaningless for the best of many. The generator shows that 1.80 is the expected best-of-100 result when nothing is there, so the number cannot be read until N is known. Scale is a session 5 question and comes after.

Why does the experiment instinct misfire on a backtest, even for a leader who reads A/B tests well?

A · Because financial data is noisier than product data
B · Because backtests use a different statistic
C · Because a backtest has no control group, no fresh draw of the past, and a rule that was chosen by reading the same path it is scored on
All three experimental supports are absent at once. Noise is real but is not the structural difference, and the statistic is just a ratio. The loop that runs backwards onto the same data is what makes a backtest a different kind of object.

A rule with no information tried once on two years of daily data reports a Sharpe ratio of 1.0. How surprising is that?

A · Not very: one estimate on 503 daily observations has a standard deviation of about 0.71 when there is nothing there, so 1.0 is about one and a half standard deviations from zero
B · Very: a Sharpe ratio of 1.0 is strong evidence of skill on any sample
C · Impossible: a rule with no information must report exactly zero
The ruler is 0.71, and the practitioner track measures it. A single 1.0 is a mild result on two years, and only if it was the only trial. Option C confuses the expected value with one draw.

Cheat sheet

The one sentence. A backtest is one history, read once, by someone who chose the rule after reading it.
The three absences. No control group, no fresh draw of the past, no design fixed before the data.
The ruler. One Sharpe estimate on two years of daily data: standard deviation about 0.71 when nothing is there.
The best of N. 1.04 at 10, 1.80 at 100, 2.33 at 1,000, on a random walk. Out of sample: zero, every time.
The paper. Seven configurations give an expected in-sample Sharpe of 1 on two years. Forty-five on five.
Question 1. How many did you try? A number, including the discarded ones.
Questions 2 and 3. What did the split respect? What did a trade cost?
The disagreement. Overfit rules earn nothing on series without memory, and may earn less than nothing on series with it. Say which.
