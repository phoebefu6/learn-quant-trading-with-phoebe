From churn model to trading desk: the transfer map

Five sessions took a backtest apart. This one turns the parts into a plan for a person. If you have shipped a churn, demand or credit model, you already own skills a research desk is short of, you are missing a handful of methods that are routine there, and you carry a few habits that report a finding on a world with nothing in it. Part 0

Marlow Street, the week it hires from a data team

Marlow Street is a constructed desk: four researchers, one head who came from a product data team herself, and the backtested Sharpe ratio of 1.8 that five sessions have now questioned from every side. The desk has budget for two hires, and the strongest candidates are an analyst, an engineer and a data scientist from conventional data teams. The head's question is not whether they are good. It is which of their habits will make the desk better on day one, which methods they will have to learn, and which of their reflexes will quietly produce a 1.8 on nothing. That is the transfer map, and every column of it has a mechanism that can be drawn.

LiveThree columns, one pipeline3 min▶

A list of skills is easy to write and hard to use, because it does not say where a skill matters. So the map is drawn against the six steps every strategy passes through on its way from an idea to money: the data as it stood on each day, the features built from it, the split that separates fitting from scoring, the search that selects one rule from many, the costs charged on every change of position, and the size it goes live at.

  • Carries over. Skills a conventional analyst, engineer or scientist already has that are worth more here than where they were learned.
  • Must learn. Methods that are routine on a research desk and rare on a data team: purged time-ordered validation, the deflated Sharpe ratio, costs as turnover times basis points, sizing by the median path, point-in-time data.
  • Must unlearn. Habits that are good practice on a churn model and actively dangerous here, because on this kind of data each one reports a finding where there is none.

Session 5 named the five contrasts that break the old playbook. This session is what a person does about them.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Transfer map. Three columns, carries over, must learn, must unlearn, filled for one person and one role.
  • Point in time. Data exactly as it stood on the day it is used: the series that existed then, with the values known then. The opposite is the table as it looks today.
  • As-of join. A join that attaches the latest value known at or before a timestamp, never after it. The data team's own tool against lookahead.
  • Purge. Dropping training observations whose label span overlaps a test observation's span. Session 4 drew why.
  • Embargo. Dropping a further stretch of training observations right after a test block. Taught as described in the literature; the generator cannot show its effect.
  • Deflated Sharpe ratio. A test of a Sharpe ratio against the expected maximum of N trials rather than against zero (Bailey and Lopez de Prado 2014).
  • Turnover. How much the position changes per day. A cost is charged on the change.
  • Official source. The author's or the publisher's own copy, free to read. Anything else is marked not official and checked against the published version.
  • Constructed. Marlow Street, its hires and every figure from the generator are invented so that the truth is known. No figure describes a real market.
Part 1 · covers what carries over

Five skills that are worth more here

9 min live

The map first, then the left column. Read the figure a row at a time: each row is one step a strategy passes through, and the three cells beside it are what a data person brings to that step, what is new at it, and what to drop there. The left column is fuller than most people expect. Time-series data handling, feature engineering, validation discipline, experiment hygiene and the habit of reading a metric with its error bar all transfer, and several are scarcer on a desk than on a data team. The dashed steel loop is the reason the right column exists: the search reads the same history again.

LiveTime-series data skills and feature engineering4 min▶

If you have written a SQL query that attaches a customer's attributes as they stood on the date of an event, rather than as they stand today, you already own the most valuable data skill on a desk. The as-of join, a lag built only from rows before the timestamp, a window that closes yesterday: these stop lookahead at the source, and session 4 drew why they matter. Dataframe libraries with a time index do the same in memory. The skill is the discipline of the timestamp, not the tool.

Feature engineering carries over almost whole: lags, rolling windows, ratios, and above all the leak hunt. The general method lives in Leakage: the session; the time-ordered classes were session 4. What changes is the price of one leak. A momentum rule whose signal is shifted by a single day, so that it includes the day it trades, reads a Sharpe ratio of 6.11 on a world with no alpha at all, against 0.02 when built honestly (200 worlds). A tabular leak usually costs a few points of accuracy; a time leak manufactures a strategy.

Real world

Marlow Street's first hire (constructed) came from a retail analytics team. In her first week she rebuilt the research table with as-of joins and asked a question nobody on the desk had asked: was every series in the table alive on the day it was used, or only alive today? It was the second. The generator's version of that mistake: keep only those of 50 zero-alpha series that did not end below -20 percent, about 9.5 of them dropped, and an equal-weight hold on the survivors reads 1.51, against -0.01 on all 50 as they stood (100 trials). She did not need finance to see it. She needed her old habit, pointed at a new table.

LiveValidation discipline and evaluation metrics3 min▶

Cross-validation discipline carries over as an instinct: a holdout nobody touched, a metric chosen before the run, a model compared with a baseline rather than admired on its own. Rolling-origin evaluation of a forecast is taught properly in Backtesting and metrics and transfers directly. What is new here is that the object evaluated is a strategy picked from many, and that overlapping observations need a purge, which is Part 2.

Evaluation metrics transfer as a habit of asking for the error bar. The Sharpe ratio is a metric like any other, and one estimate of it on two years of daily data has a spread of 0.71 by formula (the square root of 252 over 503) and 0.70 measured over 200 null worlds. A scientist who already reads an AUC with its confidence interval will read a single Sharpe of 1.0 as about one and a half spreads from nothing, which is the right reading, and only if it was the only trial.

LiveExperiment hygiene, which is worth more here than anywhere2 min▶

Seeds, a log of every run, a reference implementation that must agree with the production one: these are the habits a data team most often has and a research desk most often lacks. Every number on these pages comes from a seeded generator whose browser engine and an independent Python reference agree to 3.3e-13 on all 635 canon numbers, and the generator checks six properties of itself each time a page loads. That is ordinary hygiene, and it is what makes every figure checkable by anyone.

Multiple-testing instincts come with it. Anyone who has applied a Bonferroni correction or controlled a false discovery rate across the metrics of an A/B test already knows the question that matters most: how many were tried. On a desk the count of trials is an input to the deflated Sharpe ratio, and a trial log is the only place that number lives. Comparing a candidate with a challenger carries over too; here the challenger is a thousand random rules.

Five methods a data team does not yet carry

10 min live

The middle column is short and specific. None of it is exotic mathematics; each item is a method that exists because of one mechanism in the previous five sessions. The figure draws two of them where they cut: the purge and the embargo on a time-ordered split, and the hurdle that rises with the number of trials. The strip underneath holds the other three with the number each one is worth on the generator.

LiveTime-ordered validation and point-in-time data3 min▶

Purge and embargo. A walk-forward split keeps the test period after the training period. When each observation's label covers several days, a training observation near the boundary can share days with a test observation, so the purge drops every training observation whose label span overlaps a test span, and the embargo drops a further stretch right after each test block to absorb serial correlation. That is how Lopez de Prado describes them in chapter 7 of Advances in Financial Machine Learning (Wiley 2018); the description here is reported from the book's chapter outlines, and the book itself is described, not quoted.

On the generator, with 100 rules, nothing real and five-day holds, shuffled folds with no purge read 0.65 out of sample; blocked folds with a purge read 0.06 (100 worlds each). With one-day holds the shuffled split leaks nothing visible, 0.10 against -0.05 for walk-forward. Purging a shuffled split leaves only 141 of about 600 training observations; purging a blocked split costs four. The embargo changes nothing measurable on this world, because the purge already handles the overlap; it is taught as the literature describes it and the bench cannot show its effect.

Point-in-time data. Survivorship and lookahead are both the data as it looks today standing in for the data as it stood. Survivors only read 1.51 on 50 zero-alpha series where the point-in-time universe reads -0.01 (100 trials); a signal shifted one day reads 6.11 where the honest one reads 0.02 (200 worlds). The catch for lookahead is a correlation an honest position cannot have: 0.358 with the return of the day it trades, against -0.001 when built honestly.

LiveMultiple testing and the deflated Sharpe ratio3 min▶

A data team corrects for multiple comparisons with a Bonferroni factor or a false discovery rate. The desk's version tests the best Sharpe ratio against the expected maximum of N trials with nothing in them, rather than against zero. Bailey and Lopez de Prado (2014) built the deflated Sharpe ratio to correct "for two leading sources of performance inflation: Selection bias under multiple testing and non-Normally distributed returns". On the generator the best of N rules on nothing reads 1.04, 1.80 and 2.33 for N of 10, 100 and 1,000, the hurdle from their equation 1 is 1.07, 1.80 and 2.31, and the deflated test passes in 0 of 200 worlds at every N above 1.

The price is power. A real Sharpe of 1.5 hidden among 100 random rules on three years is almost never confirmed: it passes the deflated test in 0 of 200 worlds in ladder B, 1.5 percent of 200 in ladder B2 and 3 of 100 in ladder B3. Every rule that did pass, on every rung of ladder B, was the real one. The only cure is independent history: at 24 years the same 1.5 passes in 94 of 100. Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) reach the same place from another road, arguing after counting 316 factors in 313 papers that "A new factor needs to clear a much higher hurdle, with a t-statistic greater than 3.0".

LiveCosts, turnover, sizing and drawdown2 min▶

Costs are turnover times basis points. A cost is charged on every change of position. On the generator the position flips on about 20 percent of days, so turnover is about 0.41 units a day and each 5 basis points costs about 0.32 of annualised Sharpe. A real 1.0 reads 1.05 out of sample with no cost, is gone by 15 basis points and reads -0.23 at 20 (200 worlds). A data team rarely meets a metric that a per-action charge can turn negative; a desk meets one every day.

Size by the median path. The same real 1.0 compounded over three years: the median ends at 1.57 at leverage 1, 4.80 at 6 and 1.45 at 12, while the mean climbs from 1.61 to 14.57 to 71.48, and 98.5 percent of paths have lost half at some point by leverage 6 (200 paths each). Growth peaks at 6, the full-Kelly fraction for this world. A credit team will recognise the move from expected loss to the loss the institution must survive.

Real world

Marlow Street's second hire (constructed), a data scientist, ran the desk's 1.8 through a deflated test in her first week. The researcher's log showed about a hundred rules tried on two years. On the generator the hurdle for the best of 100 on two years is 1.80, so the 1.8 was the hurdle, not a margin over it. She did not call the rule worthless. She wrote that the backtest could not tell a real edge from the search, and asked what independent history the desk could wait for.

Live"Shuffle the folds" and "more features is fine"3 min▶

Shuffle the folds. Random k-fold is the default in every tabular toolkit, and on independent rows it is correct. On returns whose labels span five days, a shuffled fold puts the days either side of a test observation into training, and the rule is scored on days it was fitted on. With 100 rules and nothing real it reads 0.65 out of sample; blocked and purged folds read 0.06 (100 worlds each). The replacement is not exotic: time-ordered folds with the overlap purged, which session 4 drew and practitioner session 5 builds.

More features is fine. On a churn model a regularised learner shrugs off a few hundred extra columns. On a desk every feature variant, window length and parameter setting is one more trial in the search, and the search is what the deflated test charges for. A real 1.5 tried alone reads 1.48 out of sample; the same real 1.5 among 100 random rules is the one kept in only 40.5 percent of worlds, and whatever was kept reads 0.72 (200 worlds). The extra candidates did not add information, they added competitors. Replace the habit with a trial log: every variant counted, N written beside the headline.

Live"The best validation score wins" and "accuracy is the goal"2 min▶

The best validation score wins. A model-selection loop that keeps the top validation score is a best-of-N search, and on a series with no structure the top score is the expected maximum of noise. The best of 100 rules reads 1.80 in sample and -0.06 afterwards (200 worlds); a naive test calls it a discovery in 99.5 percent of worlds and the deflated test in none. Keep the loop, change the bar: test the winner against the best of N, and expect to say "not proven" far more often.

Accuracy is the goal. A churn model is judged on how often it is right. A strategy is judged on what is left after paying for every change of mind, at a size the typical path survives. A real 1.0 reads 1.05 gross and -0.23 at 20 basis points per flip (200 worlds), and at leverage 12 the mean ends at 71.48 while the median ends at 1.45 (200 paths). The replacement objective is net of costs, sized by the median and the drawdown, never the gross mean.

Live"A backtest is evidence"3 min▶

A data team treats a good holdout score as evidence because the holdout can be redrawn: next month brings new customers. A backtest's holdout is one stretch of one history, and it is consumed by being read; the next rule tried on it is in sample again. Its inputs can be quietly wrong in ways a table never shows: survivors only read 1.51 on nothing (100 trials), and a one-day shift reads 6.11 (200 worlds). And the target does not hold still the way a customer does. McLean and Pontiff, in The Journal of Finance 71(1), 2016, measure returns across 97 published predictors "26% lower out-of-sample and 58% lower post-publication".

The replacement is to treat a backtest as a hypothesis and to name what would confirm it: independent history. A real 1.5 among 100 passes the deflated test in 3 of 100 trials on three years, 6 on six, 45 on twelve and 94 on twenty-four (ladder B3). That ladder is the honest answer to "how long until we know".

Real world

Marlow Street's third candidate (constructed), an engineer, presented his take-home exercise: a backtest with a clean pipeline, seeded runs and a beautiful report. The head asked one question: "What would you need to see to believe this?" He answered "a good out-of-sample score", which was the habit. The answer she hired him for came a minute later: "Data I have not looked at yet, and a count of everything I tried before this."

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Zero or worse? Bailey, Borwein, Lopez de Prado and Zhu (2014) hold that "in the absence of memory, there is no reason to expect overfitting to induce negative performance", and that with memory a compensation effect makes the overfit rule more likely to be selected and then to underperform. The generator has no memory, so overfit rules earn zero here, not less than zero. Say "earn nothing, or worse if the series has memory", never "lose money".

Which hurdle? Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) argue for a t-statistic above 3.0 and add that there are good reasons to expect even 3.0 is too low. Bailey and Lopez de Prado (2014) deflate against the expected maximum of N and call the two approaches complementary. A transfer map does not have to pick one; it has to carry both and say which was used.

How much decay? McLean and Pontiff's published figures are 26 and 58 percent, in The Journal of Finance, 2016. Earlier drafts of the same study are reported to have given other figures; the estimate moved as the sample grew. Quote the published numbers with the journal and the year, and treat any decay assumption in a pitch as a choice to be stated.

Part 4 · build-along

Your own transfer map, and ninety days of reading

15 min hands on

No code. You fill the three columns for yourself, in the variant that matches the job you hold now, with evidence from your own work in every cell. Then you turn the middle column into a ladder of free reading you can actually finish. The figure is the card to copy: three role variants on top, the 90-day ladder underneath.

Pick your variant and copy the blank map

Draw three columns, CARRIES OVER, MUST LEARN and MUST UNLEARN, against the six steps of the pipeline in Part 1. Then pick the role you hold now. An analyst usually brings time-aware SQL and metric reporting; an engineer brings pipelines, seeds and point-in-time joins; a scientist brings validation and multiple-testing instincts. The variant decides which cells you will fill first, not which ones you may skip.

Fill CARRIES OVER with evidence, not adjectives

For each skill you claim, write the last time you used it and on what: "built as-of joins for a monthly churn snapshot", "kept a seed and a run log for a pricing experiment". A skill with no example goes in the middle column instead. Aim for four or five cells, each tied to the step where it lands.

Fill MUST LEARN in the order your role needs it

Five methods: purged time-ordered validation, the deflated Sharpe ratio, costs as turnover times basis points, sizing by the median path, and point-in-time data. Number them. An analyst usually starts with costs, because a gross figure is the thing an analyst is most often asked to report; an engineer with the purge; a scientist with the deflated test. Beside each, write the practitioner session that builds it.

Fill MUST UNLEARN with a habit you used last month

Of the five, shuffle the folds, more features is fine, the best validation score wins, accuracy is the goal and a backtest is evidence, pick the two you actually practise. Run each in the box above and write down the number it printed beside the habit. A habit with its price written next to it is much easier to drop.

Turn the middle column into a 90-day ladder

Copy the three rungs from the figure, keep the official markers, and put a date on each "do" line. Swap the order of the readings if your role needs it, but keep one written output per rung. If a source is marked not official, note what to check it against: for McLean and Pontiff, 97 predictors, 26 and 58 percent.

Show the map to someone who will disagree

A colleague on a research desk, in model risk, or the person who would review your first pitch. Ask them to move one item between columns. The item they move is the one your map was most wrong about, and it goes to the top of the first rung.

The habit to keep. Arrive with your old skills and your old questions, and leave your old defaults at the door. Every habit in the right column was a good habit somewhere else, and each one prints a number on a world with nothing in it. Write the price beside the habit, count every trial, and read one rung a month from sources whose copy you can trust.

Check yourself

Three questions

A scientist joining Marlow Street validates a rule with shuffled five-fold cross-validation on five-day returns and reports a positive out-of-sample Sharpe ratio. Which column of the transfer map does that habit belong in?

A · Carries over: k-fold cross-validation is the same method everywhere
B · Must unlearn: on overlapping observations, shuffled folds with no purge read 0.65 on a world with nothing, against 0.06 for blocked purged folds (100 worlds each)
C · Must learn: the result stands, and only an embargo needs adding
Validation discipline carries over; shuffling time order does not. The shuffled split scores the rule on days it was fitted on, and the leak appears only when labels overlap. The embargo adds nothing measurable on this world; the purge is what removes the leak.

Which habit from a data team is worth more on a research desk than where it was learned?

A · Keeping the model with the best validation score
B · Reporting the gross figure and leaving costs to someone else
C · Experiment hygiene: seeds, a log of every trial and a reference run that must agree, because the trial count is an input to the deflated test
The best validation score is a best-of-N search: 1.80 in sample and -0.06 after on nothing. A gross figure hides costs that turn a real 1.0 into -0.23 at 20 basis points. A trial log is the only place N lives, and without N no headline can be read.

An engineer drafts a 90-day reading plan. Which plan matches the ladder?

A · Free sources marked official or not: Sharpe 1994 from the author's copy, the 2014 Notices paper from the publisher, Harvey, Liu and Zhu from the author's copy, and McLean and Pontiff marked not official and checked against the published figures
B · Buy the 2018 book first and work only from quoted passages of it
C · Start from whichever online posts report the highest backtested Sharpe ratios
The ladder is free and says which copy is official. The 2018 book is not free and sits outside it. A list of the highest backtested figures is a list of the best of N, which is the habit the ladder exists to unlearn.
After the leader track

Homework

Twenty minutes now, then one rung a month.

Finish the map and date the first rung

Complete every cell you can support with an example, put a date on the first rung's "do" line, and keep the map where you will see it. Re-read it at day 30 and move anything that changed column.

Price one habit on your own work

Take one result your team reported in the last quarter and ask the right column's five questions of it. Write which habit was in play and what the generator says that habit is worth on nothing. If none was, write that down too; it is a finding.

Review one pitch at day 90

Use session 5's five questions on a real or constructed strategy pitch, with your transfer map beside it. The review is a judgement on a research claim, never a view on any market.
