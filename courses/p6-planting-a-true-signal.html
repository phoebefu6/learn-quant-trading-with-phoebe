Planting a true signal

Session 4 wrote the deflated test and read its cost off a table. Tonight runs the experiment yourself. The generator lets you put a real rule into the world on purpose, so the right answer is known before the method is asked. Plant it, turn three dials (how strong it is, how many random rules stand around it, how many years of data there are), and watch two things: whether a fake rule ever gets through, and how often the real one does. Then the harder question, the one a desk actually has to answer: what to do when the test you trust refuses something you know is real. The data is synthetic throughout, and nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Marlow Street hides the answer, then asks the question

Marlow Street is a constructed research desk, four people and a head who has been handed a backtest with an annualised Sharpe ratio of 1.8 and asked whether to fund it. After session 4 the desk has a test it trusts on nothing: against the expected best of N, false passes go to zero. What it has never seen is the test facing something real. The only way to see that is to build a world where something real is known to be there, and the generator can do it with one argument. Everything tonight is synthetic data, and nothing here is investment advice.

★ What you walk out with tonight. An exact account of how the engine plants a real rule: one constant, chosen so the rule earns the Sharpe ratio you asked for, and one strategy slot handed the world's hidden signal. Three planted ladders driven live, with their trial counts: strength (200 worlds a rung), crowd (200) and history (100). Two facts that belong together: the false discovery rate after deflation is 0.000 on every planted rung, and a real 1.5 among 100 on three years almost never passes. The minimum backtest length from the 2014 Notices paper, and why independent history is the one cure that cannot be manufactured. And a habit: never read one run of a ladder as its mean.

LiveWhy plant a signal you already know is there4 min▶

A test is two promises. It should refuse what is not there, and it should find what is. Session 4 checked the first promise on worlds with nothing in them, and the deflated test kept it perfectly. The second promise cannot be checked on a real series, because on a real series nobody knows which rule, if any, is real. A planted world is the only place where a refusal can be scored as right or wrong.

So tonight every world has exactly one real rule in it, with a strength you choose, standing among rules that know nothing. That turns the deflated test from a formula into an instrument with a measured hit rate and a measured false-alarm rate, and those two numbers are what a desk needs before it trusts the instrument with money.

Real world

Marlow Street's head, constructed like the rest of the desk, asked a question nobody had asked of the deflated test: if the 1.8 were real, would this test have said so? The researcher could not answer it, because every check so far had been on worlds where the right answer was "no". The head asked for a world where the answer was "yes", and for the test's score on it, before the 1.8 was discussed again.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Planted rule. Strategy 0 in a planted world. It is handed the world's hidden signal, so it is the one rule with a real edge. Every other rule is a random AR(1) signal that knows nothing.
  • True Sharpe. The annualised Sharpe ratio the planted rule earns in expectation. It is the dial trueSR; the generator solves for the constant c that delivers it.
  • Kept. The share of worlds in which the rule with the best in-sample Sharpe ratio was the planted one. The selection gate's hit rate.
  • Drowned. A world where a noise rule out-scored the planted one in-sample, so the real rule never reached the test.
  • Deflated pass. The kept rule's probabilistic Sharpe ratio against SR*, the expected best of N on nothing, reaches 0.95.
  • Power. The share of worlds in which the real rule is kept and passes. Here, because every pass was the planted rule, it equals the deflated pass share.
  • FDR after deflation. Of the worlds where the deflated test passed, the share where the rule that passed was not the planted one.
  • Seed range. The block of world seeds a rung averages over. Two rungs with the same settings but different seed ranges are two independent measurements of one quantity.

Part 1 · covers planting and the strength ladder

One steel rule in a pile of 99, and the two places it gets lost

12 min live

Planting is two lines of the engine and no cleverness. What it buys is a world where every outcome can be scored. Put one real rule among 99 random ones, keep the best in-sample, and run the deflated test on whatever was kept. The real rule can be lost twice: at the selection gate, when a noise rule out-scores it, and at the bar, when it is kept but cannot clear SR*. Ladder B turns the real rule's strength from 0.5 to 3.0 and counts both losses.

LiveWhat planting does, line by line4 min▶

The world. makeWorld always draws the hidden signal s first: an AR(1) with phi 0.8, scaled so its variance is one. Then it draws the returns, r[t] = sigma (c s[t-1] + e[t]) / sqrt(1 + c^2), with sigma 0.01 and e a fresh standard normal. The division keeps daily volatility at one percent whatever c is. With trueSR at 0, c is 0 and s is drawn and ignored: that is session 1's null.

The constant. A rule trading the sign of s earns sigma times c times the average size of s, over sqrt(1 + c^2), each day, with a daily standard deviation of about sigma. The average size of a standard normal is sqrt(2/pi). So the engine solves c sqrt(2/pi) / sqrt(1 + c^2) = trueSR / sqrt(252) for c, which is why the planted rule has the requested annualised Sharpe ratio in expectation, not in any one world.

The plant. makeStrategies with plantIndex: 0 copies the world's hidden signal into slot 0 and fills the other slots with independent AR(1) signals from seed s + 1000. trial sets the plant whenever trueSR is not zero. Nothing else changes: same pnl, same split of 503 training and 252 test observations, same selection, same two tests. The only difference between a planted world and a null world is which signal sits in slot 0.

How weak "real" is. Constructed arithmetic from the same formula: a real 1.5 is a per-day Sharpe ratio of 1.5 over sqrt(252), about 0.09, which for a plus-or-minus-one position is also roughly its correlation with tomorrow's return. A rule that is genuinely right is right by a correlation of 0.09. That is why it can be drowned.

LiveLadder B: five strengths, 200 worlds each4 min▶

One planted rule among 100, walk-forward 503 / 252, 200 worlds per rung. Read across: best in-sample Sharpe ratio, what that kept rule earned on the 252 test days, the share of worlds where it was the planted rule, and the deflated pass share.

  • Real 0.5. Best in-sample 1.77, out of sample -0.03; kept 2.0 percent; deflated passes 0.
  • Real 1.0. 1.81, then 0.12; kept 14 percent; passes 0.
  • Real 1.5. 1.93, then 0.46; kept 32.5 percent; passes 0.
  • Real 2.0. 2.15, then 1.09; kept 57 percent; passes 3.0 percent.
  • Real 3.0. 2.94, then 2.71; kept 90.5 percent; passes 34.5 percent.

The false discovery rate after deflation is 0.000 on every rung: every world that passed, passed the planted rule. On the first three rungs that zero means there were no passes to be wrong about, which is a weaker kind of zero and worth saying out loud.

Now read the first two columns together. The best in-sample figure barely moves from 0.5 to 1.5 (1.77 to 1.93), because below about 2 the best of 100 is mostly the best of the noise, and the noise sets the number. The out-of-sample column is the one that tells the rungs apart.

Real world

Marlow Street's researcher, constructed, noticed that the desk's 1.8 sat between the real-1.0 rung (1.81) and the real-1.5 rung (1.93) on this ladder, and also next to the 1.80 that the best of 100 reports on nothing at all in session 3. Three different truths, one in-sample number. The head's note on the memo was short: the in-sample figure cannot tell these worlds apart, so it cannot be the reason to fund.

LiveWhy the out-of-sample column is a mixture3 min▶

The kept rule is the planted one in some worlds and a noise rule in the rest. A noise rule earns about zero out of sample here, and the planted rule earns its true Sharpe ratio. So the out-of-sample mean should be close to the kept share times the true value. Constructed arithmetic against the measured column: 0.325 times 1.5 is 0.49, measured 0.46; 0.57 times 2.0 is 1.14, measured 1.09; 0.905 times 3.0 is 2.72, measured 2.71.

That is a useful way to read any out-of-sample figure after a selection: it is a blend of "picked the real thing" and "picked the luckiest noise", weighted by how often each happened. A low out-of-sample number does not say the real rule is weak. It may say the real rule was rarely the one kept.

The blend works this cleanly because the generator has no memory. The 2014 Notices paper shows that with memory, a rule selected for in-sample luck can do worse than zero afterwards, through what it calls a compensation effect. This bench cannot show that case. Read every "earns about zero" here as "earns nothing, or worse if the series has memory, which this bench does not model".

Self-studyWhat the plant is not3 min▶

The planted rule is the kindest real edge there could be. It is stationary: its Sharpe ratio is the same in year one and year three. It is free to trade until session 7 adds a cost. And nobody else is trading it away. A rule found in a real series has none of those guarantees.

The decay of published rules is measured, and the measurement moved as its sample grew. McLean and Pontiff, in the published version (The Journal of Finance, 2016), studied 97 variables and report portfolio returns 26 percent lower out of sample and 58 percent lower after publication. Earlier drafts of the same study reported other figures, which is itself a lesson about single estimates. The planted rule here decays by zero percent, so every power figure on this page is a best case.

Part 2 · covers crowd and history

Two more dials: the rivals around it and the years under it

12 min live

Hold the real rule at 1.5 and turn the other two dials. Ladder B2 grows the crowd from 1 rule to 1,000 on three years (200 worlds a rung). Ladder B3 holds the crowd at 100 and grows the history from 3 years to 24 (100 worlds a rung). One dial sinks the real rule fast. The other lifts it, slowly, and it is the only dial on the bench that a desk cannot simply turn.

LiveB2: the crowd drowns it3 min▶

A real 1.5 on three years, 200 worlds a rung, with a growing number of random rivals.

  • Alone, N = 1. Kept 100 percent (there is nothing else), out of sample 1.48, passes 65.5 percent. No deflation is possible, so this is ordinary power on two years of training data.
  • N = 10. Kept 68 percent, out of sample 1.03, passes 3.0 percent.
  • N = 100. Kept 40.5 percent, out of sample 0.72, passes 1.5 percent.
  • N = 1,000. Kept 13 percent, out of sample 0.20, passes 1.5 percent.

Two different things fall. The kept share falls steadily, because each new rival is one more chance for noise to out-score the real rule. The pass share falls off a cliff at once, from 65.5 to 3.0 percent with only nine rivals, because the bar rises from zero to SR* the moment there is anything to be the best of. At N = 1,000 the real 1.5 was in the pile all along, and the kept rule earned 0.20.

LiveB3: history lifts it, and cannot be manufactured4 min▶

A real 1.5 among 100, 100 worlds a rung, with more years. Best in-sample falls toward the truth as the noise's best shrinks: 2.03, 1.61, 1.53, 1.50. Out of sample rises toward it: 0.68, 0.93, 1.42, 1.51. Kept: 39, 56, 95 and 100 percent. Deflated passes: 3, 6, 45 and 94 percent, at 3, 6, 12 and 24 years.

The mechanism is the width of the bell. One estimate's noise shrinks like one over the square root of the sample length, so the best of 100 noise rules comes down toward zero while the real 1.5 stays 1.5. The 2014 Notices paper turns that into a rule, the minimum backtest length: MinBTL < 2 ln N / E[max]^2 (Theorem 2), the years of data needed before the best of N independent tries on nothing is expected to stay below a Sharpe ratio of E[max]. The 2 ln N comes from sqrt(2 ln N), which the paper gives as an upper bound, not an estimate. Its worked example: with five years of data, no more than 45 independent configurations should be tried.

Constructed arithmetic with the measured form instead of the bound: session 3's equation 1 value for N = 100 is 2.531 standard deviations, so keeping the expected best of 100 noise rules at 1.0 annualised needs about 2.531 squared, 6.4 years of training data alone.

And the history has to be independent. Resampling the same three years, slicing them finer or reusing them under a new split gives more rows, not more information; the noise in those rows is the same noise. New history arrives at one year per year, which is why the cure is real and slow.

Real world

Marlow Street's researcher, constructed, proposed bootstrapping the three years into thirty to "get the B3 effect for free". The head asked what a bootstrap adds that the three years did not contain. Nothing: B3's worlds are longer because the generator drew more independent days, and a resample draws no new days at all. The proposal went into the log as a tried idea, which is also a configuration, and N went up by one.

LiveOne run is not the mean: three readings of one setting2 min▶

Ladder B's real-1.5 rung, ladder B2 at N = 100 and ladder B3 at 3 years are the same setting: a real 1.5 among 100 on three years, walk-forward 503 / 252. They use three different seed ranges. Their deflated pass shares read 0 of 200 worlds, 1.5 percent of 200 and 3 percent of 100. Their kept shares read 32.5, 40.5 and 39 percent; out of sample 0.46, 0.72 and 0.68.

None of them is wrong and none of them is the answer. Each is a mean over a finite set of worlds, and a mean has noise of its own. So the honest summary is not "never" and not "1.5 percent": it is "almost never", a few percent at most, with three runs to show the spread. The professional norm is to quote a rate with its trial count, and when two runs disagree, to report both rather than pick the one that tells the better story. Picking would be selection again, one level up.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree: should a desk lower the bar to recover power?4 min▶

The case for a high bar, or a higher one. Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) counted 313 papers and 316 factors and concluded that a new factor needs a t-statistic greater than 3.0, a p-value of 0.27 percent, and they argue there are good reasons to expect that 3.0 is too low. On this view a refusal is cheap and a funded false rule is expensive, and the searches behind published results are larger than anyone reports.

The case that the bar has a price. The deflated test kept its promise on every planted rung, 0.000 false discoveries, and paid for it in power: a real 1.5 among 100 on three years almost never passes. A desk whose instrument refuses a real 1.5 in nearly every world funds nothing, and learns nothing either. Bailey and Lopez de Prado (2014) do not set the two thresholds against each other: they call the Benjamini-Hochberg style threshold and the DSR threshold complementary and encourage computing both.

What is open. Lowering the bar trades false discoveries for power, and the right trade depends on what a missed real rule costs this desk against what a funded false one costs. That is a loss function, not a statistical fact, and the literature has not settled it. The canon does not measure a lowered bar either; the homework does it by hand and its numbers are yours alone. What every side agrees on is the reporting: state the N, the SR*, the sample length and both verdicts, so a reader can apply their own bar.

Part 3 · build-along

Plant, run, read: the three ladders by hand

18 min hands on

Open this page's console. The engine is loaded as QT. Six steps: plant a rule and check it is the hidden signal, run single worlds and see how much one world swings, run ladder B's canon row, run the same setting from two other seed ranges, then the two heavy rungs that each take several seconds. Single worlds are for checking the engine; only the rungs are measurements.

Plant a rule and check it is the hidden signal

Seed 50016 is the first world of ladder B's real-1.5 rung. Slot 0 should equal the hidden signal value for value (a copy, so === on the arrays would say false). Then compute c from the engine's formula and the correlation between the planted position and the next day's return. The c involves no random number and prints the same everywhere; the correlation is one world's draw around about 0.09. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

consolevar w = QT.makeWorld({ trueSR: 1.5, seed: 50016 }); var xs = QT.makeStrategies(w, { N: 100, plantIndex: 0, seed: w.seed + 1000 }); var sPer = 1.5 / Math.sqrt(252), c = sPer / Math.sqrt(2 / Math.PI - sPer * sPer); var pos = w.signal.slice(0, 755).map(function (v) { return v >= 0 ? 1 : -1; }); [xs[0].every(function (v, t) { return v === w.signal[t]; }), c.toFixed(4), QT.corr(pos, w.returns.slice(1)).toFixed(3)]

Run five single worlds and watch them swing

QT.trial runs the whole experiment once: plant, 100 rules, keep the best on 503 days, test on 252, both verdicts. Look at the second column (1 if the kept rule was the planted one) and how far the in-sample and out-of-sample figures move from world to world. Do not average these by eye; that is what the next step is for. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

console[50016, 50017, 50018, 50019, 50020].map(function (s) { var t = QT.trial({ N: 100, trueSR: 1.5, method: "walkforward", seed: s }); return [s, t.pickedPlant, t.isSR.toFixed(2), t.oosSR.toFixed(2), t.deflatedDiscovery]; })

Run the canon row: ladder B at a real 1.5

ladderRung repeats the trial over seeds 50016 to 50215 and averages: 200 worlds of 100 rules, about a second. It prints best in-sample 1.93, out of sample 0.46, kept 0.325, deflated passes 0.000 and FDR after deflation 0.000. Change 1.5 and the seed0 together (50000 plus ten times the true Sharpe) to print any other row of the strength ladder. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

consolevar r = QT.ladderRung({ N: 100, trueSR: 1.5, method: "walkforward" }, 200, 50015); [r.isSR.toFixed(2), r.oosSR.toFixed(2), r.pickedPlant.toFixed(3), r.deflatedDiscovery.toFixed(3), r.fdrAfterDeflation.toFixed(3)]

Run the same setting from two other seed ranges

Ladder B2 at N = 100 (seed0 60100, 200 worlds) and ladder B3 at 756 days (seed0 65756, 100 worlds) are the same experiment as step 3. They print deflated passes 0.015 and 0.030 and kept shares 0.405 and 0.390. Put them beside step 3's 0.000 and 0.325: that spread is what a ladder's own noise looks like. The habit to keep. Before trusting a test with a decision, plant the answer and score the test on it: how often it lets noise through, and how often it keeps the real thing. Quote both with their trial counts, and when two runs of the same setting disagree, quote both runs.

Check yourself

Three questions

On ladder B's real-1.5 rung (200 worlds) the kept rule earns 0.46 out of sample, although the planted rule's true Sharpe ratio is 1.5. Why?

A · The deflated test shrinks the kept rule's out-of-sample Sharpe ratio
B · The kept rule is the planted one in 32.5 percent of worlds and a noise rule in the rest, which earns about zero here, so the mean is close to 0.325 times 1.5
C · The planted signal decays after the training period in this generator
The out-of-sample figure is a blend of "kept the real rule" and "kept the luckiest noise". The generator is stationary and has no memory, so the real rule earns its 1.5 and a noise rule earns about zero; 0.325 times 1.5 is 0.49 against a measured 0.46. The test does not touch the out-of-sample days at all.

A real 1.5 among 100 on three years passes the deflated test in 0 of 200 worlds in ladder B, 1.5 percent of 200 in B2 and 3 percent of 100 in B3. What is the right reading?

A · One of the three ladders has a bug, since the setting is identical
B · The true pass rate is exactly 1.5 percent, the middle value
C · They are three seed ranges for the same setting, each a mean over a finite number of worlds; the honest summary is "almost never", a few percent at most, quoted with the trial counts
A rate measured over 100 or 200 worlds carries noise of its own. Choosing the run that reads best would be selection again, one level up. Report the spread and the counts.

Marlow Street wants a real 1.5 among 100 to pass the deflated test more often without letting noise through. Which lever does the canon show working?

A · More independent history: from 3 to 24 years the pass share rises from 3 to 94 percent of 100 worlds, with every pass the planted rule
B · Bootstrapping the same three years into thirty to get more observations
C · Trying more rules, so that the real one has more chances to be kept
More rules lower the kept share and raise the bar (B2). A resample adds rows but no new days, so its noise is the noise already there. Only independent history narrows the bell of noise estimates, which is what ladder B3 shows and what the minimum backtest length formalises.

Take this with you

Cheat sheet

The one sentence. Plant the answer before trusting a test, and score it twice: noise let through, and real rules kept.
The plant. Slot 0 gets the hidden signal; c is solved so its expected annualised Sharpe ratio is trueSR. A real 1.5 is a daily correlation of about 0.09.
The promise kept. FDR after deflation 0.000 on every planted rung of B, B2 and B3: every pass was the planted rule.
The two losses. Drowned at the gate (a real 1.5 among 100 is kept 32.5 percent of 200 worlds) and refused at the bar (passes 0 of those 200).
The crowd. A real 1.5 passes 65.5 percent alone and 3.0 percent among 10 (200 worlds). The bar jumps the moment there is anything to be the best of.
The history. Among 100: 3, 6, 45 and 94 percent at 3, 6, 12 and 24 years (100 worlds). MinBTL < 2 ln N / E[max]^2; five years, at most 45 configurations.
The mixture. Out of sample after selection is roughly the kept share times the true Sharpe ratio, plus noise that earns about zero here.
The spread. One setting, three seed ranges: 0, 1.5 and 3 percent. Say "almost never" and quote every run with its trial count.
