Survivorship and lookahead, planted and caught

Sessions 3 to 7 asked what a number was the best of, whether its held-out days were unseen, and what was left after it paid to trade. Every one of those questions assumed the data itself was honest. Tonight breaks that assumption on purpose, twice. The generator drops the series that fell below a floor, and a five-day momentum rule reads tomorrow through one index slip. Neither defect needs any alpha to manufacture a large Sharpe ratio, and both are caught by tests so cheap there is no excuse for skipping them. The data is synthetic throughout, and nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Marlow Street's data, not its rule

Marlow Street is a constructed research desk, four people and a head who has been handed a backtest with a Sharpe ratio of 1.8 and asked whether to fund it. So far every session has interrogated the rule: how many were tried, how the split was made, what a trade costs. Tonight interrogates the data the rule was run on. Two questions, each with a mechanism of its own: which series were in the test, and when could each input first have been known? The way to learn what a wrong answer does is to plant one in a world with nothing in it and measure the damage.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. Two defects planted in a zero-alpha generator and measured with their trial counts. Survivorship: drop the series that ended below -20 percent and the equal-weight survivors read a Sharpe ratio of 1.51, where the full universe of 50 reads -0.01, over 100 trials of 504 days. Lookahead: slip a five-day momentum rule by one index so its window includes the day it trades, and 0.02 becomes 6.11, over 200 worlds. Two catches: the correlation of the position with the day it trades (-0.001 honest, 0.358 slipped), and one structural test for both defects, which is to cut the future off, recompute and compare. And the habit of running both catches on every backtest, not only the suspicious ones.

LiveWhy plant a defect on purpose4 min▶

A defect found in a real backtest arrives with no size attached. You know the number is wrong; you do not know by how much, or whether a fix moved it the right distance. A defect planted in a generator arrives with its truth known: every series has zero alpha, so any Sharpe ratio above noise was made by the defect and nothing else. That is the same move session 1 made with the null, applied to the data pipeline instead of the method.

Planting also tests the catch. A test that has never been shown a planted defect has never been seen to fire, and a test that cannot fire looks exactly like a clean result. Tonight every catch is run on the planted world first, where it must fail, and on the honest world, where it must pass.

Real world

Marlow Street's head, constructed like the rest of the desk, had two questions on the 1.8 that sessions 3 to 7 never answered. Which series were in the universe on the first day of the backtest? And for each input to the rule, what was the first moment it could have been known? The researcher answered the second with confidence and the first with "the current list". Tonight measures what each of those answers can be worth.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Universe. The set of series a backtest is allowed to hold on a given day. Here: 50 zero-alpha series of 504 days.
  • Survivorship. Testing only the series that are still on the list at the end. The ones that failed along the way were in the universe at the start and are missing from the test.
  • Point in time. A value as it was known on a given day, not as it is known now. A point-in-time universe is the list as it stood before the first trade.
  • Floor. The rule that removes a series: here, a 504-day total return below -20 percent.
  • Lookahead. A position on day t that uses information from day t+1 or later, the day it is about to earn.
  • Index slip. The commonest way lookahead gets in: an off-by-one in a window or a shift, so r[t - k] becomes r[t - k + 1].
  • Lag assertion. A test that recomputes each day's signal from the history up to that day only and checks nothing changed.
  • Catch. A test that fails on the planted world and passes on the honest one. A test never shown a planted defect is not yet a catch.

Sessions 1 and 2 built the null and the strategy; 3 and 4 measured selection and corrected for it; 5 made the split respect time; 6 planted a real signal; 7 charged it a cost. Part 1 · covers survivorship, planted and measured

Fifty series with nothing in them, and a list drawn up at the end

12 min live

The plant is one line. Make 50 zero-alpha series of 504 days each, add up each series' returns, and keep only those whose total ended above -20 percent. Then hold the survivors in equal weight from day one and score them. Nothing about any series changed; only the list did, and the list was drawn up with the whole history in hand.

LiveReading the survivorship rows4 min▶

Every one of the 50 series has a true Sharpe ratio of zero. Held all together in equal weight from day one, which is the point-in-time universe, they read -0.01 averaged over 100 trials: nothing, measured correctly. Drop the ones whose 504-day total ended below -20 percent, 9.5 of 50 on average, and hold the rest in equal weight from day one. The same series, minus the losers, read 1.51.

That 1.51 is not a small bias on top of a real number. It is a large number made from nothing, and it is close to the 1.8 on Marlow Street's desk. No rule was selected, no parameter was tuned, nothing was traded. The only decision was which rows went into the test, and it was made with the answer in hand.

Real world

Marlow Street's researcher, constructed, had built the 1.8 on a file of series downloaded once and kept. Asked which series were in the universe on the first day of the backtest, the researcher realised the file only knew which series existed on the day it was downloaded. The head did not assume the worst and did not assume it away: she asked for the 1.8 recomputed on the list as it stood before day one, and for the gap between the two to be reported beside it.

LiveThe catch: a point-in-time universe4 min▶

The fix is to hold, on each day, only the series that were known to be in the universe on that day, with their dates of joining and leaving. On this generator that is all 50 from day one, because no series has failed yet before the first trade. The catch is the test that proves the fix was applied: recompute the universe using only data available before day one, and assert it equals the list the backtest used. If the two lists differ, the list read the future.

On the planted world the assertion fails every time, by the 9.5 series on average the floor removed. On the point-in-time world it passes, because the list does not depend on any return yet to happen. It costs one recomputation and one comparison. It needs no zero-alpha world and no statistics, so it runs unchanged on a real backtest.

One honest limit: the generator removes a series only at the end, cleanly. A real record drops series in the middle of the history, sometimes after a final loss the record never captured. What return to book for a series on the day it leaves is a real question that this generator never asks, so no figure here speaks to it.

Self-studyWhy the survivors earn with no alpha, by arithmetic4 min▶

How many fall below the floor. A 504-day total of daily returns with a standard deviation of 0.01 has a standard deviation of 0.01 times the square root of 504, about 0.224. A floor of -0.2 sits about 0.89 standard deviations below zero, and about 19 percent of a normal sits below that. By arithmetic that is about 9.3 of 50; the measured mean is 9.5 over 100 trials.

Why the rest have a positive mean. Cut the left tail off a bell centred on zero and the mean of what remains is above zero. Each survivor's total is, on average, a little positive, not because the series was good but because the bad outcomes were removed from the sample.

Why a little positive becomes a Sharpe ratio of 1.5. The survivors are held in equal weight, about 40 of them, and their daily noise is independent. Averaging 40 independent series divides the daily standard deviation by roughly the square root of 40, while the small positive mean stays. A small mean over a much smaller standard deviation is a large ratio. The arithmetic lands near 1.5, and the measured mean is 1.51. A wider universe would make the illusion larger, not smaller, which is the opposite of what most people expect from diversification.

Nothing in the generator gives a survivor any edge after the list is drawn: each series' future returns are independent of its past by construction. The engine does not run the survivors forward, so no forward figure is quoted here.

Part 2 · covers lookahead, planted and measured

A five-day rule that reads tomorrow through one index

12 min live

The honest rule is plain momentum: at the close of day t, take the mean of the last five daily returns, days t-4 to t, and hold its sign for day t+1. On a random walk that earns nothing. The plant moves the window one day forward, so it covers days t-3 to t+1. One index, one character in the code, and the window now contains the very return the position is about to earn.

LiveReading the lookahead rows4 min▶

Switch the box above to the honest window. Over 200 zero-alpha worlds the momentum rule reads a Sharpe ratio of 0.02: nothing, as it should, because past returns of a random walk say nothing about the next one. Switch to the slipped window and the same rule on the same 200 worlds reads 6.11.

A 6.11 is loud on purpose. Nobody who has done session 3 would believe a Sharpe ratio of 6 on three years of daily data, and that is the danger in teaching lookahead only at full volume. The slip planted here puts the whole of the traded day's return into the window at a weight of one fifth. A real leak is often quieter: a value read a little early, a field that is only sometimes filled in after the fact. A quiet leak inflates a figure by an amount that looks like a modest, believable edge, and nobody's eyebrows go up. That is why the catch must be a test, not a judgement about whether the number looks too good.

LiveThe catch: correlate the position with the day it trades4 min▶

A position decided at the close of day t cannot know day t+1's return. In a zero-alpha world that means its correlation with day t+1's return must be zero up to noise, and the honest rule's is -0.001 averaged over 200 worlds. The slipped rule's is 0.358. That third column of the box is the catch: it fires on the plant and stays quiet on the honest rule.

The fourth column looks similar and is not a test. The correlation with the day before is 0.358 for the honest rule and 0.355 for the slipped one, because day t sits inside both windows: it is the rule reading its own inputs, which is allowed. A reviewer who checks the wrong column will see the same number on both rules and wave the leak through.

The correlation catch has a limit worth saying aloud. It works cleanly here because the world has no alpha, so any correlation with the traded day is a leak. On a real series with a real edge, an honest position is supposed to correlate a little with the next return; that is what an edge is. The statistic then cannot separate a small edge from a small leak. Part 3 builds the structural catch that does not care whether there is alpha at all.

Real world

Marlow Street's researcher, constructed, computed the position against the traded-day return on the 1.8 in an afternoon. It came back close to zero, and the head was careful about what that meant: it rules out the full one-day slip, the loudest form, and nothing quieter. She asked for the lag assertion as well, run over the whole history, before writing "lookahead: checked" on the proposal.

Self-studyWhy 0.358 and 6.11, by arithmetic4 min▶

The correlation. The position is the sign of a sum of five independent normal returns. The correlation between that sign and any one of the five is the square root of 2 over pi, divided by the square root of 5: about 0.357. That is why 0.358 appears for the honest rule against the day before (day t is one of its five) and for the slipped rule against the traded day (day t+1 is one of its five). The slipped rule's 0.355 against the day before is the same quantity, since day t is still inside its window.

The Sharpe ratio. Each observation is the position times day t+1's return. Its mean is 0.357 times the daily standard deviation and its standard deviation is about 0.934 times it, so the ratio per day is about 0.38. Annualised by the square root of 252 that is about 6.1 by arithmetic; the measured mean over 200 worlds is 6.11.

The noise floor. On one world of 754 observations, an honest correlation has a standard deviation of roughly one over the square root of 754, about 0.036. A one-day slip at 0.358 is ten of those out and impossible to miss. A leak a tenth that size would sit inside the noise of a single backtest, which is the arithmetic behind "a quiet leak passes the statistic".

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Statistical catches or structural ones? One camp prefers statistical alarms, the traded-day correlation among them, because they run on the output alone and need no access to the pipeline that built the signal. The other camp holds that an alarm only catches leaks loud enough to rise above noise, and insists on structural tests: every input timestamped with the moment it became known, and every signal recomputed from truncated history. The structural camp is right that a quiet leak slips under the alarm; the statistical camp is right that a truncation test only checks the code path, and a figure stored in the record with the wrong date passes it untouched. That second case, restatement, is a leak in the data rather than in the code, and the generator here never revises a figure, so nothing tonight measures it. Both camps agree neither test alone is enough; which to require first is a judgement for the team, not a result.

How far back must a point-in-time universe go? The generator shows that the mechanism exists and that, with a -20 percent floor on 50 zero-alpha series, it is large. It cannot say how large it is on any real record, because that depends on how many series failed, how badly, how far back the record of failures reaches, and what return was booked on the day each one left. Reviewers differ on how much effort rebuilding the full history deserves when part of the record is missing. Treat the size as an empirical question about a real record, never as a constant carried over from this page.

Part 3 · build-along

Plant each defect, measure the lie, add the catch

18 min hands on

Open this page's console. The engine is loaded as QT. The steps plant survivorship by hand on the seeds of ladder F's first trial, plant the slip by hand on ladder G's first world, run both canon rungs, and then write the one structural catch that works on both: cut the future off, recompute, compare. Plain JavaScript; the same lines translate to Python one for one.

Plant survivorship: 50 series and a floor

These are the seeds of ladder F's first trial, 100,001 to 100,050, each a zero-alpha world of 504 days. Add up each series' returns and keep those whose total ended above -0.2. Your numbers will match for the same seed, but this is one trial, one draw, and its count of survivors is not the canon mean.

consolevar all = []; for (var m = 0; m < 50; m++) all.push(QT.makeWorld({ days: 504, trueSR: 0, seed: 100000 + m + 1 }).returns); var total = function (r) { return r.reduce(function (a, b) { return a + b; }, 0); }; var survivors = all.filter(function (r) { return total(r) > -0.2; }); [all.length, survivors.length]

Measure the inflated Sharpe ratio, then the honest one

Average the rows day by day into an equal-weight series, then score it with the same moments and annualise as every other session. The first figure is the survivors held from day one, the second is all 50. On this one trial the gap is large; your numbers will match for the same seed, and the next step gives the mean over 100 trials.

consolevar avg = function (rows) { var y = []; for (var t = 0; t < 504; t++) { var s = 0; rows.forEach(function (r) { s += r[t]; }); y.push(s / rows.length); } return y; }; var sr = function (rows) { return QT.annualise(QT.moments(avg(rows)).sr, 1).toFixed(2); }; [sr(survivors), sr(all)]

Run ladder F at the canon seeds

survivorshipRung repeats the two steps above over 100 trials, each with its own 50 seeds, from a seed0 of 100,000. It prints 9.5 dropped on average, 1.51 on the survivors and -0.01 on the point-in-time universe. Your numbers will match for the same seed, exactly; the call takes a fraction of a second.

consolevar f = QT.survivorshipRung({ M: 50, floor: -0.2, days: 504 }, 100, 100000); [f.meanDropped.toFixed(1), f.survivorsSR.toFixed(2), f.pointInTimeSR.toFixed(2)]

Plant lookahead: one index slip in a five-day rule

Seed 110,001 is ladder G's first world, 756 days of zero alpha. mom takes the mean of five returns ending at day t minus shift, so a shift of 0 is honest and a shift of -1 is the slip. pnl holds the sign of the signal at t for day t+1. Then compute the catch: the position's correlation with the day it trades. Your numbers will match for the same seed; one world, so expect the slipped figures near the canon and the honest ones within noise of zero, not equal to the means.

consolevar w = QT.makeWorld({ trueSR: 0, seed: 110001 }); function mom(r, shift) { var s = []; for (var t = 0; t < r.length; t++) { var a = 0, n = 0; for (var k = 0; k < 5; k++) { var i = t - k - shift; if (i >= 0 && i < r.length) { a += r[i]; n++; } } s.push(n ? a / n : 0); } return s; } var honest = mom(w.returns, 0), slipped = mom(w.returns, -1); var traded = function (sig) { var p = [], r1 = []; for (var t = 1; t < w.days - 1; t++) { p.push(sig[t] >= 0 ? 1 : -1); r1.push(w.returns[t + 1]); } return QT.corr(p, r1).toFixed(3); }; [honest, slipped].map(function (x) { return [QT.annualise(QT.moments(QT.pnl(w, x, { h: 1 })).sr, 1).toFixed(2), traded(x)]; })

Run ladder G at the canon seeds

lookaheadRung runs the same rule over 200 worlds from a seed0 of 110,000, honest and slipped. It prints 0.02, -0.001 and 0.358 for the honest rule, and 6.11, 0.358 and 0.355 for the slipped one: Sharpe ratio, correlation with the traded day, correlation with the day before. Your numbers will match for the same seed.

console[0, -1].map(function (s) { var g = QT.lookaheadRung(s, 200, 110000); return [s, g.meanSR.toFixed(2), g.corrWithTradedDay.toFixed(3), g.corrWithPastDay.toFixed(3)]; })

Add the structural catch for both

Cut the future off, recompute, compare. For the universe: build the list from data known before day one and compare it with the list the backtest used. For the signal: at a spread of days, recompute sig[t] from the history up to t only and compare it with the value the backtest used. Both are pass or fail, the same on every world: the planted list differs and the point-in-time one does not; the slipped signal fails and the honest one passes. Nothing here needs zero alpha, which is why these lines belong in every backtest.

consolevar listOn = function (rows, upTo) { return rows.map(function (r, m) { return total(r.slice(0, upTo)) > -0.2 ? m : -1; }).filter(function (m) { return m >= 0; }); }; var lagOK = function (build) { for (var t = 5; t < 750; t += 25) { if (build(w.returns)[t] !== build(w.returns.slice(0, t + 1))[t]) return false; } return true; }; [listOn(all, 0).length, survivors.length, lagOK(function (r) { return mom(r, 0); }), lagOK(function (r) { return mom(r, -1); })]

The habit to keep. Two assertions run on every backtest before anyone reads its Sharpe ratio: the universe recomputed from data known before day one equals the universe used, and every signal recomputed from history cut at its own day equals the signal used. Show each one a planted defect once, so you have seen it fail. A catch you have never seen fail is a hope, not a test.

Check yourself

Three questions

Fifty zero-alpha series are filtered to drop any whose 504-day total ended below -20 percent, and the survivors held in equal weight from day one read a Sharpe ratio of 1.51 over 100 trials. What catches it?

A · A lower floor, so that fewer series are dropped and the bias shrinks
B · Building the universe from the list known before day one, and asserting that a list recomputed from data available then equals the list the backtest used
C · A longer history, which averages the survivors' luck away
The 1.51 is made by drawing up the list with the whole history in hand; the full universe of 50 reads -0.01. A different floor only changes how much is cut, and a longer history leaves a list drawn at the end just as wrong. The catch is the point-in-time list and the assertion that proves it was used.

An honest five-day momentum rule and the same rule slipped one day are run over 200 zero-alpha worlds. Which reading catches the slip?

A · The position's correlation with the day it trades: -0.001 honest against 0.358 slipped, because a position decided at the close of t cannot see day t+1
B · The position's correlation with the day before: 0.358 honest against 0.355 slipped
C · Neither; only the Sharpe ratio can tell them apart
Day t sits inside both windows, so the day-before column is the rule reading its own inputs and is the same for both. The traded-day column moves from zero to 0.358. The Sharpe ratio does move, 0.02 to 6.11, but a quieter leak moves it to a believable number, so it cannot be the test.

Why run the truncation assertions on every backtest rather than only on the ones whose numbers look too good?

A · Because every leak produces a Sharpe ratio above 6, which is easy to spot anyway
B · Because the assertions only work on zero-alpha worlds, so they must be run while testing
C · Because a quiet leak inflates a figure into a believable edge, and the assertions cost one recompute and one comparison, need no zero-alpha world and answer pass or fail
The planted slip is loud on purpose; real ones are often a fraction of a day and produce numbers nobody questions. A test that runs only when a number looks suspicious misses exactly those. The truncation test does not depend on alpha being zero, so it runs on real backtests unchanged.
Before session 9

Homework

About forty minutes, in the console or in Python.

Move the floor and predict first

Before running anything, write down which way the survivors' Sharpe ratio should move if the floor drops to -40 percent, and which way if it rises to -10 percent, using the cut-tail arithmetic from Part 1. Then run survivorshipRung at each floor over 100 trials at a seed0 of your own. Your numbers will differ from any canon figure; check the direction against your prediction and write one sentence on why.

Make the leak quiet and test both catches

Write a version of mom that adds day t+1's return at a weight of one tenth instead of one fifth. Over 200 worlds at a seed0 of your own, measure its Sharpe ratio and its correlation with the traded day; your numbers will differ. Then ask the two questions that matter: on a single world, would the correlation sit outside a noise floor of about 0.036? And does lagOK still fail it?

Audit one backtest you have seen

Take any backtest you have been shown, or one of your own. Write down how its universe was built and on what date, and for each input to its rule, the first moment that input could have been known. Mark each one passed, failed or unanswered. Session 9 turns to the size of the position, where the mean and the median of the same rule part ways.

Take this with you

Cheat sheet

The one sentence. Neither defect needs alpha to look like alpha; both are caught by cutting the future off and checking that nothing changed.
Survivorship. Drop the losers at the end: survivors 1.51, all 50 -0.01, 9.5 of 50 dropped. 100 trials of 504 days.
Why it works. A cut tail leaves a positive mean; averaging about 40 independent series shrinks the noise under it.
Lookahead. Slip a five-day momentum window one day: 0.02 becomes 6.11. 200 worlds.
The column that catches it. Position against the traded day: -0.001 honest, 0.358 slipped. Not the day before.
The structural catch. Recompute the universe before day one and each signal from history up to its day. Any change is a leak.
The quiet leak. A one-day slip is loud on purpose. A small one looks like a modest edge, so test always, not when suspicious.
The limit. No restatement, no mid-history departures, no quiet leaks in the generator. Say so when quoting it.
