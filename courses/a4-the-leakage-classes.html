The leakage classes

Selection is one way a backtest flatters itself. Leakage is the other: information reaching the rule from a time it could not have seen. In a time-ordered series it arrives by four routes, and each route has a mechanism of its own. A rule reads the day it trades. A figure revised after the fact is used as if it had been known then. The universe is chosen at the end, so the losers were never in it. A shuffled split puts tomorrow's days in today's training set. Marlow Street, a constructed four-person research desk, is about to fund a backtest with a Sharpe ratio of 1.8, and tonight its head audits that number for all four. Marlow Street, the week the 1.8 goes to audit

Marlow Street is a constructed desk: four researchers, one head, a budget for two strategies a year, and a backtested Sharpe ratio of 1.8 on the table. Session 3 settled how many rules were tried and what that does to the number. A second question is still open, and it is independent of the first: even if only one rule had been tried, could that rule have seen something it would not have known on the day it traded? A backtest that leaks does not need any selection to report a large number. The generator behind every session plants each leak on purpose, so the size of each one is measured on a world where the truth is known, and the catch for each is checked against it.

LiveWhy time-ordered leakage has classes of its own3 min▶

A data team already knows leakage as a general idea: a feature that carries the answer, a column filled in after the outcome, a preprocessing step fitted on the whole table. That general case, target leakage on a tabular model, is taught properly in Leakage: the session, and the hunt for a leak inside one model is The leak hunt. Neither is retaught here.

What is different about a backtest is that every row has a time on it, and the rule is only honest if, at each time, it uses nothing from later. That one requirement breaks in four distinct places: in the rule's own inputs, in the record those inputs were read from, in the list of series the rule was allowed to trade, and in the way the history was split for testing. Each break has its own mechanism, its own size and its own catch, which is why they are taught as classes rather than as one warning.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Knowable. A value is knowable at a time if it had been published, recorded and could have been read by then. The date a value describes is not the date it became knowable.
  • Lookahead. A rule whose position on a day uses data from that day's own return or later. The cleanest form is a signal shifted by one day.
  • Restatement. A figure revised after it was first published. Using the revised value on a date before the revision existed is lookahead through the record, not through the rule.
  • Point-in-time. A record that keeps every value as it was known on each date, with the date each version became known. The opposite of today's corrected record.
  • Survivorship. Testing on the series that still exist, or still qualify, today. The ones that failed along the way were in the universe at the start and are missing from the test.
  • Overlapping observations. When each observation earns the next five days, neighbouring observations share four of those days.
  • Purge and embargo. Purging drops training observations whose span touches a test observation's span; the embargo also drops a few training observations straight after each test block. Both are described in Lopez de Prado (2018), chapter 7, as the catalogue descriptions report it.
  • Constructed. Marlow Street and every figure here come from a generator whose truth is known. No figure describes a real market.
Part 1 · covers lookahead and restatement

What was knowable at the close of day t

15 min live

Both leaks in this Part break the same line: the close of day t, the moment the position for day t+1 is decided. Lookahead breaks it inside the rule, by letting the rule read day t+1 before trading it. Restatement breaks it inside the record, by pasting a figure that arrived later back onto day t. The figure draws the line, the honest rule that stays behind it, and both ways across.

LiveLookahead: the rule that reads the day it trades5 min▶

Take a plain five-day momentum rule on a random walk with no alpha: at the close of each day, average the last five returns, go long if the average is positive and short if it is negative, and hold for one day. Built honestly, from days up to and including t, it earns what a rule with no information should earn. Over 200 generated worlds the annualised Sharpe ratio is 0.02.

Now shift the signal by one day, so the five-day window ends at t+1 instead of t. Nothing else changes: same rule, same world, same code apart from one index. The window now includes the very return the position is about to earn, and over the same 200 worlds the Sharpe ratio is 6.11. A random walk has not become predictable. The rule has been allowed to read its own answer.

The shift is usually not written as a shift. It is a timestamp that means "the day this value describes" being joined to a table where it should mean "the day this value could be read", or a figure computed at the close being used to trade at that same close. One misaligned join is enough.

LiveThe catch is one correlation, and only one4 min▶

A leader cannot read every join in a pipeline. A leader can ask for one number: the correlation between the position held on each day and the return earned on that same day. In a world with no alpha, a position decided honestly at the close of t cannot be correlated with day t+1, because it has not seen it. Over 200 worlds the honest rule reads -0.001. The shifted rule reads 0.358.

There is a second correlation that looks like a test and is not one: the position against the previous day's return. It reads 0.358 for the honest rule and 0.355 for the shifted one, almost the same. That is just the momentum rule reading its own inputs, since yesterday's return is part of the five-day average either way. Asking for it proves nothing. Ask for the traded day.

Real world

Marlow Street's head (constructed) asked the researcher for the position against the traded-day return on the 1.8. The researcher had never computed it and produced it in an afternoon. It came back close to zero, which is the honest reading and not proof of skill: it rules out the full one-day shift, the loudest form of lookahead, and nothing else. The head wrote "lookahead: checked by correlation" on the proposal and moved to the next gate.

LiveRestatement: the figure that was not known then4 min▶

Many figures a rule might read are revised after they are first published: a count corrected, an estimate updated, an error fixed. The corrected version is better data, and that is exactly the trap. A backtest that reads today's corrected record is using, on each historical date, a value that did not exist on that date. The rule never looks ahead; the record has been rewritten under it.

The fix is a point-in-time record: every value kept as it was first published, with each later version stored beside it and stamped with the date it became known. The backtest then reads, on each date, the latest version knowable by that date. Today's corrected record cannot answer that question, because it only keeps the final version.

The generator behind these pages cannot show this class. Its returns are drawn once and never revised, so there is no corrected figure to paste back and no number to measure. That is said plainly rather than filled with an invented figure. How much a restatement moves a backtest depends on how large the revisions were and whether they leaned in the rule's favour, and only the real record can answer that.

Self-studyWhy the planted lookahead is so large, and the real one usually is not3 min▶

A Sharpe ratio of 6.11 on a random walk is a deliberately loud leak: the whole of the traded day's return sits inside the signal, weighted one fifth. Real lookahead is often quieter. A value read a few hours early, or a revision that only sometimes leans the rule's way, leaks a fraction of a day, and the inflation is smaller and easier to believe. The generator here plants only the full one-day shift, so it says nothing about the size of the quieter forms.

What carries over is the catch. Any leak from the traded day shows up as a correlation between position and traded-day return that a no-alpha world cannot produce. On a real series, where there may be a genuine edge, the honest correlation is no longer zero, so the reading is a comparison: compute it on a version of the rule that is known to be lagged safely, and see whether the live version reads higher. Practitioner session 8 plants and catches both leaks in code.

Part 2 · covers survivorship and shuffled splits

The universe chosen at the end, and the split that shares days

10 min live

The two leaks in this Part never touch the rule. Survivorship leaks through the list of series the rule was tested on: the list was drawn up at the end, so the series that failed along the way are missing. A shuffled split leaks through the test design: when observations overlap in time, a random fold puts a test observation's own days into its neighbours in the training set. The figure draws both.

LiveSurvivorship: the losers were never in the test4 min▶

Generate 50 series with no alpha at all, 504 days each, about two years. Then apply a rule that sounds like housekeeping: drop anything that ended below -20 percent, because those are the ones that were delisted, closed or no longer qualify. On average the rule removes 9.5 of the 50. Hold the remaining series in equal weight from the start and the Sharpe ratio is 1.51. Hold all 50, the universe as it actually stood on day one, and it is -0.01. Means over 100 trials.

Nothing about the rule is wrong in itself. What is wrong is when it was applied. A list drawn up at the end of the period knows which series failed, and testing on that list is testing with knowledge of the outcome. The honest universe is point-in-time: on each date, the series that existed and qualified on that date, including the ones that later failed.

Real world

Marlow Street's researcher (constructed) had built the 1.8 on "the current list", a file downloaded once and kept. Asked which series were in the universe on the first day of the backtest, nobody could say, because the file had no history. The head did not assume the worst and did not assume it away. She wrote "survivorship: unknown" on the proposal and asked for the list as it stood on day one, with the dates each series joined and left.

LiveShuffled splits: when neighbours share days4 min▶

Every tabular tool shuffles rows before it folds them, and on independent rows that is correct. On a backtest whose observations each earn the next five days, it is a leak. A test observation's five days are four-fifths inside the training observation on either side of it, and a random fold almost always puts those neighbours in the training set. The rule is chosen partly on the very days it is then tested on.

The size, from 100 worlds with no alpha and the best of 100 rules kept: shuffled five-fold cross-validation on five-day observations reads 0.65 out of sample, on nothing. Purge the training observations whose span touches a test span and it falls back to -0.02. With one-day observations, which do not overlap, the shuffled split reads 0.10, within trial noise of zero (about 0.07 at 100 trials) and of the walk-forward's -0.05. So the leak needs both ingredients: a shuffle and overlap.

The engine's own self test checks this property on every page load: on its smaller check, a shuffled split reads 0.62 and the purged one -0.14. When the purge was deliberately broken into a step that removes nothing, that check failed, 0.62 then 0.62, which is how anyone can know the purge is doing work.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

How far back must a point-in-time universe go, and how much does survivorship matter in practice? The generator can show that the mechanism exists and that, with a -20 percent floor on 50 zero-alpha series, it is large. It cannot say how large it is on any real universe, because that depends on how many series failed, how badly, how far back the record of failures reaches, and whether they were dropped before or after their losses. Those are facts about a real record, and none of them is in the generator. Reasonable reviewers differ on how far back a point-in-time universe must be rebuilt before the remaining gap stops mattering, and nothing measured here settles it. Treat the size as an empirical question for the real record, never as a constant carried over from this page.

Purged k-fold or walk-forward? Lopez de Prado (2018), Advances in Financial Machine Learning, Wiley, is described in catalogue listings as explaining in chapter 7 why ordinary k-fold cross-validation fails in finance and offering a purged k-fold as the solution, and as setting walk-forward beside cross-validation and a combinatorial purged version in chapter 12. On that view a purged cross-validation uses more of the history than a single walk-forward path. Others prefer walk-forward because it is the only design in which the test always follows the training in time, which is how the strategy will actually be run. On this generator a purged blocked split reads 0.06 and walk-forward -0.07 on the five-day rung, both within noise of zero, so the bench does not choose between them. Both camps agree on what matters most: never shuffle overlapping observations without a purge.

The embargo. It drops a few training observations straight after each test block, to absorb serial correlation that a purge might miss. On this generator it changes nothing measurable (-0.10 with it, 0.06 without, against a trial noise of about 0.07), because the purge already removes the overlap. That is a statement about this world, not about the embargo, which is taught as described and not shown.

Self-studyWhat a purge costs, and why the shuffled one looks worse3 min▶

Purging a blocked split, where the test fold is one contiguous stretch, costs only the few observations at its edges: 597 training observations against 601 unpurged. Purging a shuffled split is another matter. The test observations are scattered through the whole history, so almost every training observation touches one of them, and only 141 of 601 survive. On so small a sample the best of 100 rules reports 2.77 in-sample, because the maximum of many noisy estimates grows as the sample shrinks, while the out-of-sample figure stays at -0.02.

The lesson for a reviewer is that a purge is cheap on a split that respects time and ruinous on one that does not. If a pipeline needs a purge that discards three quarters of the data, the split was the wrong shape to begin with. Practitioner session 5 builds all eight rows of this comparison.

Part 3 · build-along

The leak audit

17 min hands on

No code. Marlow Street's head puts five questions to the researcher behind the 1.8, one gate per class plus the one test that catches lookahead outright. Work through them on a result your own team has presented. Every gate ends in one of three words: passed, failed or unanswered, and an unanswered gate is treated as failed until somebody answers it.

When was each input knowable?

List every input the rule reads. Beside each, write two dates: the date the value describes, and the date it could first have been read. If the backtest joins on the first date, the rule is reading values before they existed. Marlow Street's researcher found one input stamped by the day it described and published the next morning, and moved it back a day.

Which series were in the universe on day one?

Ask for the list of series as it stood on the first day of the backtest, with the date each joined and left. "Today's list" is not an answer. If the list cannot be produced, write "survivorship: unknown" and remember the generator's version of the gap: 1.51 on survivors against -0.01 on everyone, from series with no alpha at all.

Were any figures revised, and which version did the backtest read?

For each input that can be corrected after publication, ask whether the backtest read it as first published on each date or as it stands today. Only a point-in-time record can answer the first way. There is no generator number to hold this against, so the answer is a yes or a no, and a no means the gate is not yet passed.

How was the split made, and do the observations overlap?

Two questions in one. Was the test period after the training period, or were the rows shuffled? And does each observation earn more than one day, so that neighbours share days? A shuffle with overlap and no purge is the combination that lifts nothing to 0.65; ask which of the three ingredients is present.

What does the correlation of position with the traded day read?

Ask for one number: the correlation between each day's position and the return earned on that same day, computed on the rule as it was backtested and on a version deliberately lagged one extra day. If the backtested rule reads well above the lagged one, something is reading the traded day. On the generator the honest rule reads -0.001 and the shifted one 0.358.

Write one word per gate

Passed, failed or unanswered, for each of the four classes. Marlow Street's sheet read: lookahead passed after one input was moved, survivorship unanswered, restatement unanswered, split passed (walk-forward, daily observations). The head did not read the 1.8 again until the two unanswered gates were closed.

The habit to keep. Before reading any backtest number, ask what the rule could have known on the day it traded, and from which list and which version of the record. A leak needs no selection and no bad intent; one misaligned date is enough to turn a random walk into a 6.11. The number on the slide is only as honest as the dates under it.

Check yourself

Three questions

A five-day momentum rule on a random walk with no alpha reads 0.02 built honestly and 6.11 when its signal is shifted one day. Which number catches the shift?

A · The correlation of the position with the previous day's return, which is high for the shifted rule
B · The correlation of the position with the return on the day it trades: -0.001 honest against 0.358 shifted, over 200 worlds
C · The Sharpe ratio alone, since anything above 3 must be a leak
The previous-day correlation is 0.358 against 0.355, nearly the same for both, because the rule reads its own inputs either way; it is not a test. Only the traded-day correlation separates them, since an honest position cannot have seen that day. A large Sharpe ratio is a reason to look, not a catch.

Fifty series with no alpha are filtered to drop any that ended below -20 percent. The survivors held in equal weight read a Sharpe ratio of 1.51. What is the leak?

A · The list was drawn up with knowledge of how each series ended; on the universe as it stood on day one, all 50, the same holding reads -0.01 over 100 trials
B · The -20 percent floor is too strict, and a looser floor would remove the effect
C · Equal weighting is the problem; any other weighting would read near zero
The rule is applied at the end, so it removes exactly the series that went on to lose, and the test never sees them. The fix is a point-in-time universe, not a different floor or weighting. How much this matters on a real universe is an empirical question the generator cannot answer.

On the same zero-alpha world, shuffled five-fold cross-validation reads 0.65 out of sample with five-day observations but 0.10 with one-day observations. Why the difference?

A · Five-day observations have a higher true Sharpe ratio
B · Five folds is too few for daily data
C · Five-day observations overlap, so a test observation shares four of its five days with the training neighbours on each side; purging those neighbours brings it back to -0.02
The leak needs a shuffle and overlap together. With one-day observations there is no overlap and 0.10 is within trial noise of zero (about 0.07 at 100 worlds). There is no alpha in either case, so a higher true Sharpe ratio is impossible.
Before session 5

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one result your own team has reported, in any field where rows carry a date.

Run the four gates on one real result

Write one word per gate: passed, failed or unanswered. For each unanswered gate, write the name of the person who could answer it and the one artefact you would need from them: an availability date, a day-one list, a version history, a split description.

Find one date that means two things

Look through one table your team uses and find a date column that could mean either "the day this describes" or "the day this was known". Write down which it actually means and how you found out. If nobody knows, that column is where your lookahead will come from.

Bring Marlow Street's two open gates

Survivorship and restatement were left unanswered on the 1.8. Write one sentence on what the head should do if the day-one list never turns up. Session 5 adds the costs and the sizing that decide whether a clean 1.8 is still worth funding.
